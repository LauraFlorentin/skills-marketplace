# Structured Thinking: 8 Essential Consulting Frameworks

This is a working reference guide for the analytical frameworks that unlock faster problem-solving in client engagements. Each framework solves a specific consulting challenge: from turning decisions into accountability, to drilling past symptoms to root causes, to structuring persuasive communication that actually drives action.

These are not theoretical models. They are tools you use in the room, in real time, with stakeholders watching. They are designed to be applied under pressure when stakes are high and time is short.

---

## 1. What-by-When (Accountability Framework)

### Purpose
Vague agreements don't get done. "We'll improve customer experience" is not a plan. What-by-When converts fuzzy consensus into concrete commitments with owners, specific dates, and tracked dependencies. It is the framework that transforms a good meeting into actual work.

### When to Use
- Immediately after any decision, workshop, steering committee, or strategic planning session
- When you notice people leaving the room with different interpretations of what was agreed
- During post-engagement handoff to ensure the client can execute without you
- Weekly status reviews and progress tracking
- Escalation: When commitments slip and accountability gets murky

### The Structure
| Action | Owner | Deadline | Dependencies | Status |
|--------|-------|----------|--------------|--------|
| [Specific, verb-driven action] | [One person, by name] | [Specific date, MM/DD/YYYY] | [Blocked by? Enables?] | [On track / At risk / Complete] |

### Rules That Matter

**One Owner Per Action**
Do not write "Finance and Operations will align on the new policy." That is not an owner. That is a committee. Committees do not own accountability; they distribute it. You need a name: "Sarah Chen (Finance) leads alignment with Ops lead Marcus." This creates friction in the room sometimes, but that friction is productive—it forces the real decision about who is accountable.

**Dates Must Be Specific**
"Next month" is not a deadline. "ASAP" is not a deadline. "End of Q2" is not a deadline when Q2 has 13 weeks. Write MM/DD/YYYY. If a deadline is truly uncertain, write the latest defensible date and flag it as "date subject to resource availability from IT" in the dependencies column. At least it is explicit.

**Dependency Tracking**
Every action in a sequence either "enables" or is "blocked by" something else. Before you leave the room, verify that the first action has no blockers and that later actions are sequenced properly. Nothing derails a 90-day program faster than discovering in week 3 that you needed approval from the board before you could start.

**Status Tracking Cadence**
Do not create a What-by-When and never look at it again. Review it weekly. Any item overdue by 3+ days without explanation or revised deadline gets escalated to the sponsor. This is not punishment. It is early warning that something is stuck.

### Worked Example: Marketing Transformation Program

| Action | Owner | Deadline | Dependencies | Status |
|--------|-------|----------|--------------|--------|
| Conduct customer interview study (3 weeks, 15 interviews) | Julie Martinez (Marketing) | 02/28/2025 | None—start immediately | On track |
| Compile interview findings into trend report | Julie Martinez (Marketing) | 03/07/2025 | Blocked by: customer interviews complete | On track |
| Present findings and draft strategy to leadership team | Julie Martinez + VP Marketing | 03/14/2025 | Blocked by: findings report ready | On track |
| Finance to model budget impact of three strategic options | Brian Walsh (Finance) | 03/21/2025 | Blocked by: strategy options finalized | At risk—needs data from Julie by 03/17 |
| Board to approve chosen strategy and budget | CEO + CFO | 03/28/2025 | Blocked by: Finance modeling complete, Board agenda confirmed | On track |
| Marketing operations to map talent gaps against new strategy | Marcus Lee (Ops) | 04/04/2025 | Blocked by: strategy approved, org structure decisions made | Not started—waiting on board approval |
| Schedule recruiting kick-off for open roles | HR Lead Priya Desai | 04/11/2025 | Blocked by: talent mapping complete, hiring approvals in place | Not started |

### Anti-Patterns to Avoid

**Shared Ownership**: "Marketing and Sales will work together on the segmentation model." When it fails (and it will), both teams point at the other. Instead: "Sarah owns the segmentation model. She will co-design with Sales but carries the deadline."

**Vague Deadlines**: "We'll finalize the proposal soon." Soon is not a date. A client executive might hear "three weeks." The project lead might hear "two months." By the time they talk again, trust is already damaged.

**No Follow-Up Cadence**: If you build the What-by-When and walk away, it will not be maintained. Assign a program manager or sponsor to own the weekly review. Make it 15 minutes. Same time, same day, every week. By week 3, people will stop "forgetting" their deadlines.

**Circular Dependencies**: "A blocks B, B blocks C, C blocks A." This happens when multiple teams are working in parallel without clear sequencing. When you spot it, escalate immediately to the sponsor. It usually means the phases were not designed correctly.

### Integration: Feeding Into Status Reporting

A well-maintained What-by-When becomes your weekly status report. Count the number of items: on track, at risk, overdue. Track completions. When you roll this into a steering committee update, you have objective data about execution velocity. Red items automatically escalate to the committee. At-risk items show where you need help. On-track items build confidence.

Many consultants make the mistake of writing elaborate status narratives. Skip that. Show the What-by-When table. It tells the story faster and more honestly.

## 2. Crawl-Walk-Run (Phased Implementation)

### Purpose
Every transformation looks impossible if you try to build the final state all at once. Crawl-Walk-Run breaks any initiative into three maturity stages with clear success criteria and gates between them. It makes the impossible feel inevitable.

### When to Use
- Implementation roadmaps for any transformation or capability-building program
- Technology adoptions (new platform rollouts, cloud migration, system implementations)
- Organizational restructures and process redesigns
- Market expansions or new business ventures
- Skill-building initiatives across teams

### The Three Phases

**Crawl (Months 1–3): Foundation and Minimum Viable State**
- Focus: Establish the basics, prove the concept works, minimize risk
- Scope: The 80/20—the subset of features or processes that deliver 80% of the value with 20% of the complexity
- Audience: Small group, willing early adopters, or controlled pilot environment
- Success looks like: Proof that the approach works, lessons documented, team has foundational skill
- Example: A new customer journey platform goes live for one product line, not all products
- Deliverables: Minimum viable operating model, training for pilot group, 30-day feedback loop
- Typical duration: 1–3 months

**Walk (Months 3–9): Scale and Optimize**
- Focus: Broaden adoption, refine based on Crawl learnings, measure performance rigorously
- Scope: Roll out to additional teams, functions, or geographies; incorporate feedback loops
- Audience: Growing user base, including early skeptics (who are now seeing results)
- Success looks like: 60–70% adoption, measurable improvement in key metrics, processes stable
- Example: Expand the platform to three more product lines, add advanced features, optimize workflows
- Deliverables: Updated playbook based on learnings, trained super-users, performance dashboard
- Typical duration: 3–6 months

**Run (Months 9+): Full Capability, Continuous Improvement**
- Focus: Achieve full organization adoption, embed continuous improvement mindset
- Scope: Everyone is using the system or process; it is now business-as-usual
- Audience: Entire organization or target population
- Success looks like: 85%+ adoption, sustained performance improvement, competitive advantage is clear
- Example: All product lines are using the platform; the team is running quarterly optimization sprints
- Deliverables: Embedded centers of excellence, automation of routine processes, quarterly capability upgrades
- Typical duration: 6–12+ months

### Gate Criteria: Clear Permission to Move Forward

**Gate from Crawl to Walk**
Before you scale, answer these questions:
- Is the core concept proven? (Do the metrics show improvement?)
- Is the team stable? (Have turnover or absences blown the pilot apart?)
- Are there documented learnings? (Can you tell the story of what worked and what did not?)
- Is the scalable process documented? (Is it repeatable, or was it a one-off because someone heroic drove it?)

If any answer is "no," stay in Crawl. Build the missing element. Do not move to Walk hoping to fix it later.

**Gate from Walk to Run**
Before full rollout, verify:
- Has adoption reached at least 50–60%? (If it has not reached half the target audience, something is wrong. Find out what.)
- Are there measurable results? (Revenue up, cost down, cycle time shorter, quality higher—pick your metric.)
- Has the team internalized the new way of working? (Or are they just executing the process because you are watching?)
- Is the change sustainable without ongoing consulting support? (This is the true test of readiness.)

### The 80/20 of Complexity Principle

Never start with the full feature set or the complete process redesign. Identify the 20% of the new capability that delivers 80% of the value. In Walk, you add the next 30%. In Run, you add the rest. This buys you time to build organizational readiness while still demonstrating early wins.

Example: New procurement platform
- Crawl: Basic purchase-to-pay for routine office supplies (high-volume, low-complexity category)
- Walk: Expand to MRO (maintenance, repair, operations) and expand approval workflows
- Run: Add strategic sourcing, supplier management, contract tracking, analytics

The hard parts (strategic sourcing, supplier relationships) are saved for Run, when the organization has momentum and training is embedded.

### Worked Example: Digital Transformation of a Procurement Function

**Crawl (Jan–Mar 2025): Proof of Concept**
- Scope: Implement new e-procurement platform for indirect spend (office supplies, IT equipment)
- Pilot: One business unit (150 people), 8 super-users trained
- Timeline: Platform selection (2 weeks) → Configuration (4 weeks) → Pilot go-live (2 weeks) → Feedback (2 weeks)
- Key action: Build a case study showing cost savings and process time reduction
- Gate review date: 03/31/2025
- Success criteria: 70% of pilot users able to place orders without help desk support; 15% cost reduction in pilot category

**Walk (Apr–Aug 2025): Scale and Optimize**
- Scope: Roll out to all business units; add supplier catalog and real-time spend visibility
- Users: Full procurement team (30 people) + 80 decentralized requesters across all units
- Timeline: Roll-out planning (2 weeks) → Expanded training program (4 weeks) → Phased go-live across units (6 weeks) → Stabilization and optimization (6 weeks)
- Key action: Build feedback loop; hold monthly "optimization sprints" with super-users
- Gate review date: 08/31/2025
- Success criteria: 65% self-service rate; average order cycle time reduced from 5 days to 2 days

**Run (Sep 2025+): Full Capability and Continuous Improvement**
- Scope: Full process automation, supplier relationship management, strategic sourcing analytics
- Users: Entire procurement organization + all business units
- Timeline: Advanced automation build (8 weeks) → Integration with financial systems (8 weeks) → Supplier onboarding (ongoing)
- Key action: Establish center of excellence; quarterly capability releases
- Success criteria: 80%+ self-service; procurement headcount reduced by 20% through automation; supply chain cost reduced by 12%

### Common Pitfall: Executive Impatience

The most dangerous moment is the handoff from Crawl to Walk. Executives see early progress and want to skip Walk and go straight to Run. They say, "We do not have time for a slow rollout. Roll it out to everyone now."

This almost always fails. You end up with:
- Insufficient training for the full population
- Design flaws that were never caught because the pilot was too small
- Overwhelmed help desk and support teams
- Loss of credibility when the system breaks under load

Your job is to protect the gate. Show the business case for Walk: "If we roll out to the full organization now and only 40% adopt the platform, we spend the money and get no return. The two-month Walk phase costs $200K but ensures we hit the 70% adoption target we need to break even. The gate review will trigger full rollout only if Walk hits its metrics."

### Variation: Crawl-Walk-Run-Fly

For transformations with significant upside or competitive advantage, consider adding a "Fly" phase:
- **Fly (12+ months)**: Stretch goals, optimization, innovation on top of the baseline capability
- Example: Once procurement is running smoothly, Fly might be: automated supplier negotiation, AI-driven spend analytics, predictive demand modeling

Do not talk about Fly until Run is stable. But it is worth signaling to the organization that continuous improvement is the expectation, not a one-time project.

## 3. Five Whys (Root Cause Analysis)

### Purpose
Symptoms are not problems. "Customer satisfaction is down" is a symptom. The real problem might be "delivery is three days late" or "the product update broke existing workflows" or "customer support response time doubled." Five Whys drills past the symptom to the root cause—the thing you can actually fix.

### When to Use
- Problem diagnosis (anything is broken, underperforming, or failing)
- Post-mortem analysis (what went wrong, and why did we not catch it earlier?)
- Process improvement (we are doing this, but it is inefficient)
- Issue escalation (leadership is asking why something failed)
- Decision-making (should we build this ourselves or buy it?)

### The Method

Ask "why" iteratively, typically 5–7 times, until you reach something actionable (a root cause you can actually influence or fix).

**Example: Customer NPS Scores Dropped 8 Points**

| Level | Question | Answer |
|-------|----------|--------|
| Symptom | Why did NPS drop 8 points? | Customer satisfaction survey shows lower satisfaction with product quality and support response times. |
| Why 1 | Why is satisfaction with product quality lower? | Three major customer-reported bugs in the last release were not caught in testing. Feature that customers use most frequently regressed. |
| Why 2 | Why were these bugs not caught in testing? | QA team did not test the specific workflows affected by the code change. New code was merged without running the full regression test suite. |
| Why 3 | Why did QA not test those workflows? | Development team did not flag the affected workflows to QA. QA did not have a documented list of critical user paths to test. No automated regression test coverage for these workflows. |
| Why 4 | Why did Dev not communicate the risk? | New developer (hired 2 months ago) was not aware of the critical path documentation. Code review process did not catch the risk. No mandatory checklist for developers to confirm risk assessment before code merge. |
| Why 5 | Why was the developer not trained on critical paths, and why did code review miss it? | No onboarding process for engineering; critical path documentation exists but is not part of new-hire training. Code review checklist does not include "critical path analysis" as a required review step. |
| Root Cause | What is the actual problem we can fix? | Two systemic issues: (1) No engineering onboarding process to train developers on critical user paths; (2) Code review process does not require explicit critical path assessment before merge. |

Notice: You did not stop at "bugs were introduced." That would be unfair to blame the developer. You drilled to the system failures—missing training, missing process rigor—that allowed the bug to happen.

### Rules for Effective Five Whys

**Each "Why" Must Be Answered With a Fact, Not Speculation**
- Do not accept "they were not careful enough" or "people did not prioritize quality"
- Push back: "What do you mean by not careful? Show me what happened step by step."
- Facts: "The developer did not run the regression test suite." "The critical path document was not shared with the new hire." "The code review checklist did not mention critical paths."

**Branch When You Find Multiple Causes**
A single issue often has multiple causes. When you identify a branch, explore both.

Example: "The delivery was late. Why?"
- Branch A: "Supplier delayed the component shipment."
- Branch B: "Our manufacturing line was down for 3 days due to equipment failure."

Do not stop after one. Ask why both branches happened. You may find that a root cause like "insufficient supplier redundancy" or "lack of preventive maintenance" applies to both.

**Stop When You Reach Something Actionable**
Root cause analysis is not complete until you reach something you can actually do something about. If you drill all the way to "people are human" or "the market is unpredictable," you have gone too far. Back up one or two levels to find the business control point.

**Document Each Level Clearly**
Write this down as you go. Do not try to hold five levels of "why" in your head and summarize later. You will lose rigor. Show each level to the team. The act of writing forces clarity.

### When Five Whys Fails: The Ishikawa Diagram

Five Whys assumes a linear chain of cause and effect: A caused B, which caused C. But some problems have multiple independent causes (quality issues, safety incidents, customer dissatisfaction) that all contributed.

When you find that the root cause is "multiple independent failures all happened at the same time," switch to a fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram:

Categories of causes:
- People: Who was involved? What training or skills are missing?
- Process: What steps or workflows failed?
- Equipment/Technology: Did systems, tools, or infrastructure fail?
- Materials/Data: Were inputs insufficient or incorrect?
- Environment: Did external factors (timing, seasonal demand, market conditions) contribute?

Organize findings by category. This makes it easier to see if you have a "people problem" or a "process problem" or (often) both.

### Facilitation Tips

**Who Should Be in the Room**
- The person closest to the problem (the operator, the customer service rep, the project lead)
- At least one level of management (to provide context and authority to fix process issues)
- Do not invite the executive who is responsible for the failure (they will become defensive and the team will self-censor)

**How to Avoid Blame and Stay Factual**
Start the conversation: "We are looking for what happened and why the system allowed it to happen. This is not about finding who screwed up. It is about finding what is broken in our process, our tools, or our information flow that we can fix."

If someone says "John did not do X," translate it: "What process should have required John to do X? Why did not that process work?" This moves from blame to systems thinking.

**How to Stay Factual**
When you hear an explanation that feels too easy ("They just did not care enough"), press harder:
- "Walk me through what happened step by step."
- "What information did the person have at the time?"
- "What was supposed to happen, and why did not that happen?"
- "If that had happened correctly, would the outcome have been different?"

### Output Format

Document the root cause as a simple statement, not a paragraph:

**Root Cause**: "The software release process does not require QA sign-off for code that affects critical user workflows, and developers do not have a defined list of critical workflows to assess against."

**Corrective Actions**:
1. Create and maintain a "Critical Paths Document" listing the top 10 user workflows; update quarterly
2. Require developers to assess code changes against critical paths in the pull request (add to code review template)
3. Require QA sign-off on any code change affecting critical paths (update deployment process)
4. Add critical paths training to engineering onboarding (by next new hire)
5. Monthly review of critical path incidents (to spot trends and close gaps in the document)

## 4. 2x2 Matrix (Prioritization and Classification)

### Purpose
A 2x2 matrix forces you to make trade-offs instead of trying to do everything. It takes a messy list of opportunities, problems, or initiatives and maps them on two dimensions, creating a structured conversation that exposes assumptions and disagreements.

### When to Use
- Prioritization of roadmap items, projects, or capabilities
- Portfolio analysis (what to invest in, what to divest)
- Strategic options assessment (comparing different paths forward)
- Resource allocation (where to spend time and budget)
- Risk or decision-making under uncertainty

### Common Matrix Types and When to Use Each

**1. Effort vs. Impact (The Essential Prioritization Matrix)**
- X-axis: Effort (what will it take to do this? Estimate in weeks, cost, or team capacity)
- Y-axis: Impact (what return will we get? Revenue, cost savings, customer satisfaction, risk reduction)
- Best for: Roadmap planning, capability building, process improvement
- Quadrants:
  - Top-left (Low effort, high impact): **Do First** — these are the quick wins
  - Top-right (High effort, high impact): **Do Second** — these are the big bets; build momentum with quick wins first
  - Bottom-left (Low effort, low impact): **Delegate** — small maintenance work; give to junior staff for development
  - Bottom-right (High effort, low impact): **Drop** — kill these; they are resource sinks

**2. Urgency vs. Importance (Eisenhower Matrix for Prioritization)**
- X-axis: Urgency (is this time-sensitive?)
- Y-axis: Importance (does this matter to strategy or outcomes?)
- Best for: Time management, capability prioritization within a program
- Quadrants:
  - High importance, high urgency: **Do Now** — crisis mode; all hands on deck
  - High importance, low urgency: **Schedule** — strategy work, capability building; block time
  - Low importance, high urgency: **Delegate** — interruptions; do not let them take your time
  - Low importance, low urgency: **Cut** — time sinks; this is where hours disappear

**3. Feasibility vs. Desirability (Innovation and Opportunity Assessment)**
- X-axis: Feasibility (can we actually build or deliver this with our current capabilities?)
- Y-axis: Desirability (do customers want this? Does it solve a real problem? Is there market demand?)
- Best for: Innovation portfolios, new product decisions, market expansion decisions
- Quadrants:
  - Low feasibility, high desirability: **Research** — great idea, but we need to figure out how to do it
  - High feasibility, high desirability: **Fund** — do this now; it is a winner
  - High feasibility, low desirability: **Monitor** — we can do it, but nobody wants it (yet)
  - Low feasibility, low desirability: **Kill** — neither feasible nor desirable; no reason to pursue

**4. Strategic Fit vs. Financial Return (Investment Portfolio)**
- X-axis: Financial Return (what is the ROI or value creation?)
- Y-axis: Strategic Fit (does this advance our strategy or fill a gap in our portfolio?)
- Best for: Corporate portfolio review, M&A screening, capital allocation
- Quadrants:
  - Low fit, high return: **Question** — make money but not on our strategy; should we divest?
  - High fit, high return: **Core** — invest more; these are our crown jewels
  - High fit, low return: **Build** — strategically important; need to improve the financial model
  - Low fit, low return: **Divest** — neither strategic nor profitable; exit

**5. Risk vs. Reward (Decision-Making Under Uncertainty)**
- X-axis: Risk (what could go wrong? What is the downside?)
- Y-axis: Reward (what is the upside? What is the potential benefit?)
- Best for: M&A due diligence, market entry decisions, major capital projects
- Quadrants:
  - High risk, low reward: **Avoid** — do not take this bet
  - High risk, high reward: **Consider Carefully** — suitable only with strong risk mitigation plan
  - Low risk, high reward: **Do** — rare; grab these when you find them
  - Low risk, low reward: **Accept** — low priority; do if capacity allows

### How to Facilitate a 2x2 Session

**Step 1: Define the Axes Clearly**
Before you plot anything, discuss what each axis means:
- "High effort means more than 8 weeks of project management time or more than 50K in external spend"
- "High impact means annual revenue increase of 2M+, or cost savings of 500K+, or a 10-point NPS improvement"

If people have different definitions, your matrix will be meaningless. Spend 10 minutes aligning on definitions.

**Step 2: Plot Items Relative to Each Other, Not Against Absolute Standards**
You are not trying to place each item on an absolute grid. You are trying to see which items are harder than others, which are more impactful than others. Start with 2–3 anchor items everyone knows, then plot the rest relative to those anchors.

Example: "Let's start with the CRM upgrade [high effort, high impact]. Compared to that, is the API automation effort or impact higher or lower? If lower, plot it to the left and/or down."

**Step 3: The Conversation Matters More Than the Positions**
The matrix is just a conversation starter. Someone might say, "I put that initiative in the bottom right, but I think you put it in the top left. Why?" That question—and the answer—is where the thinking happens. You are surfacing disagreements about effort estimates, impact assumptions, or business priorities.

**Step 4: Label Quadrants With Action-Oriented Names**
Do not leave quadrants blank. Label them with what you will do:
- Do First
- Do Second
- Delegate
- Monitor
- Fund
- Kill
- Avoid

This forces clarity. Once something is in a quadrant, the action is clear.

### Worked Example: Product Roadmap Prioritization

**Axes**: Y = Customer Impact (measured by expected NPS lift), X = Development Effort (estimated in weeks)

```
HIGH IMPACT
    ^
    |        [Redesign onboarding]      [Build integration API]
    |          (Top-left: Do First)     (Top-right: Do Second)
    |
    |                    *
    |                *
    |        [Mobile app v2]        [Analytics dashboard]
    |        *
    |
    |    [Fix checkout bug]    [Update documentation]    [Dark mode]
    |        *                      *
    |                                            *
    |_____________________________________________________________________
LOW IMPACT    <Small effort>          <Large effort>
              (Low effort / High impact)     (High effort / Low impact)

Quadrant Actions:
- Do First: Redesign onboarding (small effort, high impact) — start immediately
- Do Second: Build integration API, Analytics dashboard (high effort, high impact) — queue for next quarter
- Delegate: Update documentation, dark mode (low effort, low impact) — assign to interns or junior developers
- Drop: Any items in the bottom-right would go here (high effort, low impact)
```

### Best Practices for 2x2 Matrices

**Calibrate With 2–3 Items First**
Do not try to plot 15 items at once. Plot 2–3 first. Get agreement on their relative positions. Then add more. This prevents the "plot creep" where everything ends up in the same quadrant because you lose calibration.

**Use Sticky Notes for Physical Sessions**
If you are facilitating in a meeting room, use sticky notes (one item per note) on a whiteboard. You can move them around. People can stand and point and argue about positioning without committing to any particular placement. This reduces defensiveness.

**Photograph and Digitize Immediately**
The moment you have consensus, photograph the board. Then transcribe it into a digital matrix that you can share (Slides, Miro, PowerPoint, even a simple table). Do this the same day. If you wait, people will reinterpret what they thought they agreed to.

**Revisit and Update**
Quarterly, at minimum. As new information comes in ("that feature took 50% longer than estimated") or the environment changes ("our biggest competitor just launched this"), your matrix becomes outdated. Update it. This keeps it a living tool, not a one-time exercise.

## 5. Storyline (Consulting Narrative Structure)

### Purpose
Data without narrative is noise. A 30-slide deck full of charts is not a recommendation. Storyline is the framework that takes your analysis and structures it into a persuasive logical flow that actually changes thinking and drives action. In consulting, your ability to communicate clearly is as important as your analytical rigor.

### Two Complementary Approaches

**SCR Framework (Situation-Complication-Resolution)**
This is the narrative arc. It tells a story with tension and resolution.

**Pyramid Principle (Minto)**
This is the logical structure. It organizes your supporting arguments around a central claim.

Together, they create communication that both moves people emotionally (the story) and convinces them logically (the structure).

### The SCR (Situation-Complication-Resolution) Framework

**Situation**
- What is the current state?
- What does everyone agree on?
- This is the baseline; it should be non-controversial
- Example: "The company has grown 30% YoY for five years, but the operating margin has contracted 2 points each year."

**Complication**
- What changed?
- What is going wrong?
- What is the tension we are trying to resolve?
- This is where you surface the problem or opportunity
- Example: "At current cost trajectory, margins will turn negative by 2027. This threatens the company's growth strategy and investor confidence."

**Resolution**
- What should we do?
- What is your recommendation?
- What is the path forward?
- This is where you propose the answer
- Example: "We recommend a three-year operating model redesign focused on automation, vendor consolidation, and process standardization, targeting a 300 basis point margin recovery."

Structure your communication so that someone can understand the situation, see why it matters (the complication), and understand what you are proposing (the resolution) in the first minute. Everything else is supporting detail.

### The Pyramid Principle (Minto)

Once you have your recommendation (the resolution), support it with 3–5 pillars of reasoning, each with evidence underneath.

**Top of the Pyramid: The Main Claim**
- State your recommendation or conclusion immediately
- This is the "so what" —the answer to the question the client is paying to have answered
- Do not make them wait until slide 20 to find out what you think
- Example: "We recommend investing 8M in digital capability over the next two years. This will generate 15M in incremental revenue and reduce unit costs by 18%."

**Second Level: The 3–5 Pillars**
- Group all your supporting points into 3–5 major buckets
- Each pillar must be mutually exclusive (they do not overlap) and collectively exhaustive (together, they make the case)
- This is MECE thinking: Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive
- Example for the digital investment recommendation:
  - Pillar 1: Market analysis shows digital-first competitors are gaining 3 points of market share annually
  - Pillar 2: Customer research reveals 40% of customers would switch for a better digital experience
  - Pillar 3: Internal capability assessment shows we have the technical foundations but lack user-centric design and agile delivery
  - Pillar 4: Financial modeling shows 8M investment pays back in 18 months through increased conversion and retention

**Third Level: Evidence**
- Under each pillar, show the data that supports it
- Use the simplest visual or data point that proves the point
- Do not show every table; show the insight
- Example under Pillar 1: a line chart showing market share trend of digital leaders vs. traditional competitors, with projection to 2027

### When to Use Storyline

**Executive Presentations**
The executive does not have time for a 30-slide technical deep dive. They need the story first (SCR), then 3–4 slides showing the key evidence (the pillars), then one slide on implications.

**Steering Committee Updates**
Your status update should follow SCR: Situation (we planned to hit X), Complication (we are running 2 weeks behind on Y), Resolution (here is how we catch up). Do not dump tables on the committee.

**Recommendation Documents**
If you are writing a 50-page recommendation, the executive summary should be SCR + one-page pillar summary. The detailed chapters can dig into methodology and evidence, but the structure should always follow the pyramid.

**Email Summaries**
"Hi Sarah, we analyzed your procurement inefficiency. You have three major issues (complication): [brief list]. We recommend addressing them in this sequence (resolution): [brief list]. I am attaching the full analysis."

### Worked Example: Transforming a 20-Slide Data Dump Into a 5-Slide Storyline

**Bad: 20-Slide Version**
Slide 1: Title
Slide 2: Project scope and objectives
Slides 3–8: Historical data on customer satisfaction, cost trends, employee engagement, market growth
Slides 9–13: Process-by-process analysis of current state
Slides 14–15: Process capability benchmarking against industry
Slides 16–18: Technical capability assessment
Slides 19: Recommendations (finally, on slide 19)
Slide 20: Implementation timeline

**Good: 5-Slide Version**

*Slide 1: Situation-Complication-Resolution (the story)*
- Situation: "We have grown 25% in revenue over three years but have not scaled operations proportionally."
- Complication: "Margins are eroding 100 bps per year due to inefficient processes and rising labor costs. At this rate, we hit zero margin by 2027."
- Resolution: "We recommend a three-year operating model redesign focused on automation and process consolidation, targeting 300 bps margin recovery."

*Slide 2: Why This Matters (Strategic Context)*
- Investors expect 22% EBITDA margins; we are trending to 18% by 2026
- Two competitors have already improved margins by 200 bps through digital transformation
- Without margin recovery, we cannot fund growth R&D or invest in next-generation capabilities

*Slide 3: Pillar 1 — The Opportunity (What's Broken)*
- Current state analysis: we have 47 manual process steps across order-to-cash; industry best practice is 12 steps
- Opportunity: 40% reduction in processing time and 25% reduction in labor cost through automation and consolidation
- Chart: Process-step comparison (current vs. best practice) and cost impact

*Slide 4: Pillar 2 — The Approach (How We Fix It)*
- Crawl (Year 1): Automate order processing for top 5 product categories (60% of volume)
- Walk (Year 2): Extend to all categories; add supply chain optimization
- Run (Year 3): Full operating model embedded; continuous improvement cadence established
- Chart: Crawl-Walk-Run timeline with quarterly milestones and expected margin impact

*Slide 5: The Case (Why This Works)*
- Financial impact: 15M in annual savings by Year 3
- Investment required: 8M (capex and consulting)
- ROI: 1.9x, payback in 18 months
- Risk: Mitigation plan in place for talent transition and change management
- Chart: 3-year financial bridge showing investment, savings, and margin trajectory

Everything is here. In 5 slides, a busy executive sees: what is happening (situation), why it matters (complication), what you recommend (resolution), what is broken that justifies the recommendation (the pillars), and why it will work (the evidence).

### Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Leading With Process or Methodology Instead of the Answer**
Bad opening: "We conducted a comprehensive diagnostic of your supply chain, including process mapping, cost analysis, capability assessment, and stakeholder interviews. Here is what we found..."

Good opening: "Your supply chain costs are 18% above industry benchmark. We found three root causes and recommend a three-phase improvement program. Here is the opportunity."

**Too Many Pillars**
If you have 7 reasons why something is true, you do not have 7 strong reasons; you have 1 strong reason and 6 weak ones. Force yourself to choose 3–4 pillars. If you have 7 findings, combine them into thematic pillars.

**Non-MECE Grouping**
Your pillars overlap or leave gaps. Ask: "If I lined these up end-to-end, do they cover all the reasons someone should care? Do any of them overlap?"

Example of bad grouping:
- Pillar 1: Market opportunity
- Pillar 2: Technical feasibility
- Pillar 3: We have the right team
- Pillar 4: Financial returns

"We have the right team" overlaps with technical feasibility. Combine them. Now you have three pillars (market, capability, financial).

### The True Test: The Headline Test

Can a busy executive get the complete message from the first slide alone? If they read only the headline and the three bullet points on Slide 1, do they understand what you are recommending and why? If the answer is no, you have not done the work yet. Rewrite.

## 6. Red-Amber-Green (RAG) Assessment

### Purpose
Executives do not have time to read long status reports. RAG assessment gives you a traffic-light view of a project, program, or portfolio's health in one glance. Green = on track, no action. Amber = at risk, requires attention. Red = off track, escalation needed.

RAG is not a color-coding exercise. It is a rigor check. Every color must be justified. Every Amber must include a mitigation plan. Every Red must include an escalation path and what action the executive needs to take.

### When to Use
- Weekly or bi-weekly status reports to the steering committee
- Portfolio reviews to determine investment prioritization
- Health checks on multiple concurrent projects
- Risk assessments across a transformation program
- Quarterly business reviews

### Standard RAG Definitions

**Green (On Track)**
- Performance is within planned tolerance
- Deliverables are on schedule
- Spend is within budget
- Quality metrics are being met
- No significant risks or issues that have not been mitigated
- No action required from leadership; continue execution

**Amber (At Risk)**
- Performance is approaching or slightly exceeding acceptable variance
- One or more key metrics is trending negative
- There is a known issue or risk that has a mitigation plan in place
- Action or decision is required from leadership, but not emergent
- There is a defined plan to bring the item back to Green
- Escalation path is clear

**Red (Off Track)**
- Performance significantly exceeds acceptable variance
- One or more critical deliverable is delayed, at serious financial risk, or at quality risk
- Known issue lacks a credible mitigation plan
- Significant leadership action or decision is required
- Risk of project failure or material impact on business
- Escalation is immediate; executive action is needed this week

### Dimensions to Assess (Select 4–6 Per Project)

For any given project, track these dimensions:

**Timeline (Schedule)**
- Are we on track to deliver by the planned date?
- Are there critical path dependencies at risk?
- Is there float in the schedule, or are we running tight?

**Budget (Cost)**
- Are we tracking to the planned spend?
- Are there overruns, and if so, are they covered by contingency?
- Is there visibility into the rest of the year?

**Scope**
- Is scope creep occurring?
- Are we adding requirements that were not planned?
- Is there agreement on what "done" means?

**Quality**
- Are deliverables meeting quality standards?
- Are defect trends improving or deteriorating?
- Is customer satisfaction with quality consistent?

**Resources**
- Do we have the right people on the team?
- Is turnover an issue?
- Are key roles filled, or is the team stretched?

**Stakeholder Satisfaction**
- Do key sponsors and users believe this is on track?
- Is there organizational alignment?
- Are there concerns that have not surfaced?

**Risk**
- Are there known risks that could impact delivery?
- Is there a plan to mitigate them?
- Has anything unexpected emerged?

### RAG Assessment Rules

**Always Include a Brief Justification for Each Color**
Do not hand the executive a matrix full of colors without explanation. Each RAG assessment must include 1–2 sentences explaining why it is that color.

Example:
- Green: Timeline is on track; delivered Phase 1 on 02/15, one week early
- Amber: Timeline still on track, but procurement delay for new servers pushed start of infrastructure setup from week 3 to week 4; contingency plan is to compress testing from 4 weeks to 3 weeks
- Red: Timeline is now at risk by 6+ weeks; critical talent departure in January forced rescoping of Phase 1; new resource not fully ramped until April

**Amber Must Include a Mitigation Plan and Owner**
Do not leave something Amber and hope it turns Green on its own. You must answer: "What are we doing to fix this, and who is responsible?"

Example: "Amber — resource constraint on data engineering. Mitigation: (1) Reallocate one analyst from Analytics to Data Engineering (effective 02/20, lead: Bob); (2) Contract additional data engineering support from [vendor] for 12 weeks (lead: Procurement); (3) Compress the Data Model phase from 8 weeks to 6 weeks by parallel-tracking model design and infrastructure build (lead: Principal)."

**Red Must Include Escalation Path and Executive Action Required**
Do not just flag Red and walk away. You must answer: "What does the executive need to do to help?"

Example: "Red — Phase 1 delivery is now 6 weeks behind; root cause is significantly higher data complexity than initial assessment. Options: (1) extend timeline by 6 weeks, pushing final delivery to July (acceptable delay, requires sponsor approval); (2) reduce scope of Phase 1 by 30% to hit original date (removes non-critical features; acceptable from business perspective, requires sponsor decision); (3) add 2 FTE and increase spend by 300K to compress the delay to 3 weeks (accelerated path; requires CFO approval). Recommendation: Option 2 (reduced scope, original timeline). Sponsor decision needed by 02/18."

**Never Jump From Red to Green in One Period**
If something was Red last week, it should be Amber this week if the mitigation plan is working. Going from Red to Green in one period signals either (1) the Red was overstated, or (2) you are not being honest about status. Incremental movement (Red → Amber → Green) is credible.

**Use Trend Arrows**
Add an arrow to each assessment to show trajectory:
- ↑ Improving (was Amber, trending to Green)
- → Stable (Amber, but mitigation plan is holding steady)
- ↓ Deteriorating (was Amber, now Red)

This tells the executive at a glance whether things are getting better or worse.

### Portfolio RAG Dashboard: Aggregating Individual Project RAGs

When you are running multiple projects, aggregate their RAGs to show portfolio health.

Example: Program with 5 projects across 5 dimensions

```
Portfolio Status as of 02/20/2025

                  Schedule   Budget   Scope   Quality   Resources
Project A:        Green ↑    Green    Green   Green     Amber →
Project B:        Amber →    Amber    Green   Amber ↓   Red ↓
Project C:        Green      Green    Amber   Green     Green
Project D:        Amber ↓    Red      Red     Amber ↓   Amber
Project E:        Green ↑    Green    Green   Green     Green

Summary:
- Overall portfolio status: Amber
- Schedule: 3 Green, 2 Amber (1 improving, 1 deteriorating)
- Budget: 3 Green, 1 Amber, 1 Red
- Scope: 4 Green, 1 Amber
- Quality: 4 Green, 1 Amber
- Resources: 3 Green, 2 Amber, 1 Red

Critical Issues (Red items):
1. Project B Resources: Vacancy in lead data scientist role (open for 8 weeks); mitigation is interim contractor (leads: HR), timeline: filled by 03/15
2. Project D Budget: Infrastructure overrun of 1.2M due to higher-than-expected cloud costs; mitigation plan approved by CFO; no timeline impact if spending controlled month-to-month going forward

At-Risk Items (Amber trending down):
1. Project B Quality: Defect rate increased 15% in last sprint; root cause is testing capacity; mitigation: pull QA analyst from Project E (acceptable, Project E has buffer)

Recommended Actions:
1. Approve Project B resource contingency (cost: 200K for interim contractor)
2. Monitor Project D monthly spend; escalation trigger if cloud spend exceeds 20K/month
3. Approve QA reallocation from Project E to Project B
```

This one-page view tells the executive: which projects are healthy, which are at risk, what the critical issues are, and what decisions or approvals you need. It is far more useful than 30 slides.

### Common Gaming: Keeping Things Green Too Long

A common mistake: managers keep projects marked "Green" long after they should be "Amber" because admitting risk feels like admitting failure. This is a trust problem.

Solution: Define objective criteria for each color in advance. Do not argue about colors during the review. Just measure against the criteria:

Example criteria for Timeline:
- Green: 0–5 days behind planned milestone
- Amber: 5–15 days behind
- Red: 15+ days behind

This removes subjectivity. If a milestone was due on 02/28 and we missed it by 12 days, that is Amber, period. No discussion.

## 7. Elevator Pitch (Rapid Communication)

### Purpose
You have 30 seconds in a hallway with the CEO. You have 60 seconds to pitch a project to a skeptical customer. You have two minutes before the call ends. Elevator pitch is the skill of distilling any recommendation, idea, or status update into 30-second, 60-second, and 2-minute versions so that you are never caught flat-footed or unprepared.

### When to Use
- Unexpected encounters with executives (hallway, elevator, parking lot)
- Opening any presentation or proposal
- Email subject lines and opening paragraphs
- Voicemails and recorded messages
- Stakeholder alignment in one-off conversations
- Leadership meetings where time is crunched

### The Three Tiers

**30 Seconds: "The Lobby to Elevator" Version**
This is the absolute minimum. One sentence: What is the insight or recommendation, and why should I care?

Formula: "We analyzed [scope] and found [key insight]. This means [implication for the executive]."

Examples:
- "We analyzed your supply chain and found you are paying 18% more per unit than competitors. This is costing you 5M annually in lost margin."
- "We surveyed 200 customers and found that 40% would switch if you offered a mobile app. This is a 200M revenue opportunity."
- "We audited the new system implementation and found the integration is incomplete. This will prevent the sales team from accessing customer history, creating a go-live risk."

Deliverable: One sentence, period. Not more.

**60 Seconds: "The Elevator Ride" Version**
This is the full story in a minute. Situation, complication, resolution (the SCR).

Formula:
1. Context (what are we talking about?): 15 seconds
2. Finding or insight: 20 seconds
3. Implication (why this matters): 15 seconds
4. Recommendation (what should you do?): 10 seconds

Example (supply chain):
"We completed a benchmarking study of your procurement function. We found you are paying 18% more per unit than best-in-class competitors and processing orders through a manual workflow that takes 5 days. If we do not change this, margins will erode another 2 points next year. We recommend a three-year digital transformation starting with automation of order processing, targeting a 300 bps margin recovery."

Time check: That is 60 seconds. You mentioned the scope (procurement), the finding (18% premium, 5-day cycle), the implication (margin erosion), and the recommendation (3-year transformation). The executive can decide in that minute if they want to hear more.

**Two Minutes: "The Coffee Queue" Version**
This is the detail level. Context, deeper findings, implication, recommendation, and next step.

Structure:
1. Context (what are we analyzing and why): 30 seconds
2. Key findings (what did we discover?): 40 seconds
3. Implications (what does this mean for the business?): 25 seconds
4. Recommended approach (how do we fix it?): 20 seconds
5. Next step (what do we need from you?): 5 seconds

Example (full 2-minute version of the supply chain pitch):
"Over the past 6 weeks, we benchmarked your procurement function against 12 direct competitors and reviewed best practices in software and automotive. Here is what we found: First, your cost per unit is 18% above the median, driven by two factors—you have 47 manual steps in order processing that best-in-class operations have automated down to 12, and you are paying premium rates on 30% of spend because you lack a vendor consolidation strategy. Second, your cycle time is 5 days from order to delivery of an order to the warehouse, compared to 2 days for your best competitors. This matters because, at your growth rate, this cost premium and cycle time inefficiency will erode another 2 points of margin by next year, which brings you below your investor targets. We recommend a three-year operating model redesign. Year 1, we automate order processing for your top five product categories, which is 60% of volume. Year 2, we extend that to all categories and consolidate vendors. By Year 3, you have embedded the new capability and are running optimization sprints quarterly. This approach targets a 300 basis point margin recovery by Year 3. What we need from you right now is to authorize a two-week detailed design phase—cost is 80K, timeline is March, and output is a detailed roadmap and business case we present to the board in April."

That 2-minute pitch covers context, findings, implications, the recommendation (with phases), and the next step. The executive has everything they need to decide if they want to move forward. You have not overwhelmed them with data; you have told them a story.

### Formulas That Work

Use these templates when you are preparing pitches:

**Formula 1: Finding + Implication**
"We analyzed [what] and found [insight]. This means [business implication]. We recommend [action] which would deliver [benefit]."

Example: "We analyzed customer churn and found that 60% of customers who leave cite poor onboarding experience. This means we are losing 3M in annual revenue to a preventable problem. We recommend a three-week redesign of the onboarding program, which could recover 2M of that annual revenue."

**Formula 2: Risk + Consequence**
"The biggest risk to [objective] is [finding]. If we do not act by [deadline], [consequence]. Here is what we propose..."

Example: "The biggest risk to the product launch is that we have only tested the new platform with 5% of our customer base. If we do not complete user testing by March, we risk launching into a major usability flaw that could impact adoption. We propose expanding the beta to 20% of customers by 02/28, with daily feedback loops built in."

**Formula 3: Three Things You Need to Know**
"Three things you need to know about [topic]: [1], [2], [3]. The most important is [X] because..."

Example: "Three things about the market expansion: First, Brazil represents a 300M market opportunity with 15% annual growth. Second, our competitors are already there; we are two years behind. Third, market barriers to entry are falling as regulations relax. The most important is that we have an 18-month window to gain market share before the market consolidates around 2–3 players."

### How to Prepare: The Writing-Backward Approach

Write the 2-minute version first. Make sure it is complete—context, finding, implication, recommendation, next step. Practice it out loud. Time it. You should be at 120 seconds.

Then cut ruthlessly to 60 seconds. What can you remove? Usually, you can remove some context (assume the executive knows the basic situation), and combine findings (pick the top 2 instead of 3).

Then cut again to 30 seconds. You are left with one key insight and one action. That is your 30-second pitch.

This backward approach ensures that all three versions are consistent and that the longer versions do not contradict the shorter ones.

### Practice Technique: Record and Listen Back

Do not just write your pitches. Speak them out loud and record yourself.

Listen back. How do you sound?
- Do you use filler words ("um," "like," "basically")? Mark them and cut them.
- Do you rush? Slow down. Executives do not want speed; they want clarity.
- Do you hedge too much? ("I think," "it seems," "we might")? Replace with confidence: "We found," "We recommend."
- Do you spend too long on context? Assume the executive knows the basics.

Most people are surprised by how much they can cut on the second listening. A 2-minute pitch often contains 30 seconds of filler and hedging.

### Anti-Patterns: What Not to Do

**Starting With Background or Methodology**
Bad: "We ran a six-week engagement where we interviewed 40 stakeholders, conducted process mapping for the order-to-cash cycle, and completed a benchmarking study against 12 competitors. Here is what we learned..."

Good: "We found that your procurement costs are 18% above industry median, driven by manual processing and vendor premium rates. Here is what this means for your margin..."

The executive does not care how you found it. They care about the finding and what to do about it.

**Using Jargon or Acronyms**
If you do not know for sure that the executive uses your jargon, use plain language.

Bad: "We recommend an RACI-aligned operating model with embedded OCAP oversight."

Good: "We recommend reorganizing so that one person is clearly accountable for each process, with monthly reviews from the board's oversight committee."

**Hedging Too Much**
Bad: "I think we might want to consider possibly exploring a potential redesign of the process, which could potentially improve margins."

Good: "We recommend redesigning the order process. This will improve margins by 2–3 points."

Executives respect clarity over caution.

## 8. Day-One Plan (Initiative Launch)

### Purpose
Every major initiative or program has a Day One. But too often, leaders spend weeks planning the details of Day One and forget to plan the first 30 days. Day-One Plan makes sure that the transition from announcement to execution is smooth, visible, and momentum-building.

### When to Use
- Program kickoffs and transformation launches
- New leader onboarding and transitions
- Post-merger integration and organizational changes
- New office or team setup
- Major process or system implementations
- Large customer engagements

### The Structure: Five Time Horizons

**Before Day 1: Pre-Work (2–4 Weeks Before Launch)**

The work that happens before anyone sees the new initiative.

- Stakeholder alignment (executive team agrees on the message)
- Internal communications plan (how will employees first hear about this?)
- Key stakeholder one-on-ones (the CEO or program lead meets with top 10 influencers to align them before the public announcement)
- Logistics (office space, tools, access, contact lists)
- Quick wins identification (what can we deliver in Week 1 that signals momentum?)
- Team assembly (who is on the core team, and are they informed and ready?)
- Decision framework (who decides what, during the transition?)

Before-Day-1 output:
- Leadership alignment memo (one page: what the initiative is, why it matters, what success looks like)
- Stakeholder pre-brief (talking points for the CEO to use in 1-on-1s)
- Communications plan (email announcement, all-hands talking points, FAQ, intranet content)
- Roles and decision rights document (who is accountable for what)
- First-week schedule (who meets with whom, in what order)

**Day 1: The Launch (First 8 Hours)**

This is the announcement and kickoff. It should be short, high-energy, and clear.

Morning:
- All-hands announcement (15 minutes): Why the initiative exists, what success looks like, who is leading it. Keep it short. People will have questions; do not try to answer all of them in the all-hands.
- CEO Q&A (30 minutes): Address the most pressing concerns. Be honest about what you do not know yet.
- Dismiss people to work (they have jobs to do; do not waste the day in meetings)

Afternoon:
- Core team kickoff (60 minutes): This is the working team only. Go deeper on objectives, first-phase scope, and week 1 deliverables. Make sure everyone is aligned.
- Leadership team sync (30 minutes): One level up from the core team. Confirm roles, dependencies, and what they need to communicate to their organizations.
- End of day: Send an email recap to all employees: what was announced, what happens next, where they can get more information.

Key principle: Day 1 is about clarity and momentum, not completeness. You are not supposed to have all the details figured out. You are supposed to have a direction and a team that is ready to dig in.

**First Week: Establish Rhythm and Norms (Days 2–5)**

The work that proves this is real and sets the operating rhythm.

Monday (immediately after announcement):
- Core team daily standup starts (15 minutes, 9 AM every day)
- Program lead meets with each core team member 1-on-1 (30 minutes each) to understand their context and concerns
- Finance and HR are pinged to understand resource constraints and hiring/budget processes

Tuesday–Thursday:
- First external stakeholder meetings (program lead meets with key customers, partners, or board members; they hear directly from leadership, not through the rumor mill)
- First week deliverables ship (a document, a dashboard, an analysis, a proposal—something visible that shows the team is working)
- All-team sync (30 minutes): What are we learning? What questions are we trying to answer? What do we need help with?

Friday:
- First weekly retrospective (30 minutes): What went well in Week 1? What would we do differently? Is the team gelling?
- Week 2 planning (30 minutes): What are the key deliverables for next week? Who is doing what?

Rhythm established:
- Daily standup: 9 AM every morning
- Weekly team sync: Friday 3 PM
- Weekly retrospective: Friday 4 PM
- 1-on-1s with core team: Every two weeks (or weekly, depending on pace)

**First 30 Days: Early Wins and Relationship Building**

By the end of 30 days, you want three things:
1. An early win (something you can point to and say "this is progress")
2. Broader team alignment (if the core team is 8 people, 40+ people across the organization should now understand what is happening)
3. Baseline metrics (what are we measuring success against?)

Week 2–3 activities:
- Expanded stakeholder interviews (program lead meets with 20+ people across the organization to understand their perspective, their concerns, and what they need)
- First phase scope finalized (based on Week 1 learning, you have now locked in what the first phase will actually be)
- Early win delivered (small, visible, high-signal deliverable; e.g., if this is a customer experience redesign, deliver the new customer service SLA; if this is a cost reduction program, identify and lock in the first 5M in savings)
- Communications update (send an email to the whole company: "Here is what the team is learning. Here is the first win. Here is what happens next.")

Week 4:
- 30-day checkpoint with sponsor (program lead presents to the CEO or program sponsor: progress, findings, any blockers)
- Team expansion (if the core team was 8 and you need 20, start bringing people on for Phase 2)
- First course correction (based on 30 days of learning, adjust the roadmap if needed)
- 60-day look ahead (plan is now visible for the next 30 days)

**First 90 Days: Major Milestones and Stakeholder Check-in**

By 90 days, the initiative is no longer "new." It should be in full execution mode.

Key outputs:
- First phase is either complete or in final stages
- Metrics are being tracked weekly (progress dashboard is live)
- Organization believes this is real and has staying power (not a consultant project that goes away)
- First major course corrections have been made (if the approach was wrong, you fixed it by week 6, not week 20)
- Broader organizational change is underway (if this was a transformation, other teams are now rethinking their own work)

90-day review:
- Present progress to the board or top leadership
- Walk through metrics: Where did we plan to be, and where are we?
- Highlight early wins and lessons learned
- Confirm the Phase 2 roadmap
- Stakeholder feedback: What are people saying about the initiative?

### Key Elements for Each Phase

**Communication Plan (Who Hears What, When, and How)**

You need multiple communication streams, not just all-hands emails:

- Employees: All-hands (quarterly), team updates (weekly from their leader), intranet content (updated weekly)
- Board/Senior Leadership: Executive steering committee (monthly), flash reports (weekly top 3 key metrics)
- Customers (if external initiative): Progress updates (quarterly), beta access if applicable (rolling basis)
- Suppliers/Partners: Briefings (as needed, often at the start and before major milestones)

Each stream has different content and cadence. Do not send board-level detail to frontline employees (they will panic about complexity); do not send 10-line executive summaries to working teams (they need more detail).

**Quick Wins (Visible, Low-Effort, High-Signal Actions)**

A quick win is something that:
- Takes 1–4 weeks to deliver (not longer)
- Requires minimal resources and external dependencies
- Is visible to the organization (not buried in a database)
- Directly supports the initiative (not tangential)

Examples:
- If you are launching a new operating model: deliver an org chart, a decision rights document, a communication plan
- If you are launching a customer experience program: deliver a new customer complaint resolution SLA, a simplified feedback process
- If you are launching a cost reduction program: deliver the list of top 20 cost savings opportunities, ranked by effort and impact
- If you are launching a digital transformation: deliver a prototype of the new interface, or a case study of an early adopter success

Quick wins are psychological. They signal: this is actually happening, the team knows what it is doing, progress is real.

**Relationship Map (Who to Meet, in What Order, What to Ask)**

At the start of a program, create a list of 20–30 key stakeholders you need to understand:

For each:
- Who: Name, title, what is their influence?
- Why: What is their stake in this initiative? What could go wrong if they do not support it?
- When: What order should you meet them? (Often: finance first, then customer facing, then operations)
- What to ask: What is their top concern? What do they need to hear to be confident? What are they worried about?

By 30 days, you should have met with all of them. By 60 days, they should feel heard.

**Decision Rights (Who Decides What)**

Spell this out explicitly:

- Program Lead decides: Scope, phase sequencing, team composition, day-to-day execution priorities
- Sponsor (CEO) decides: Budget changes >10%, timeline changes, escalations
- Steering Committee decides: Strategic direction changes, major scope changes, trade-offs between competing priorities
- Finance decides: Budget approval, hiring approvals
- Operations/HR decides: Resource allocation, team onboarding

Write it down. When a decision comes up, everyone knows where it goes.

### Worked Example: Day-One Plan for a Consulting Engagement Launch

**Before Day 1 (Week of 02/17/2025)**

Pre-work:
- Client CEO and consulting partner align on engagement objectives and success criteria (meeting: 02/17, 2 hours)
- Internal consulting team is briefed and assigned roles (core team: 6 people; meeting: 02/18, 1.5 hours)
- Kickoff agenda is planned; materials are prepared; office space is set up (02/19)
- Client senior leadership team is pre-briefed in 1-on-1s by the CEO (02/17–02/19; 5 conversations, 30 min each)

Outputs:
- Engagement objectives document (one page)
- Working team roles document (who does what, who reports to whom)
- Kickoff agenda (4-hour agenda for Feb 24)
- Stakeholder interview schedule (first 10 interviews, scheduled for Feb 24–Mar 7)

**Day 1 (Monday, February 24, 2025)**

Morning (9 AM–12 PM): Kickoff All-Hands
- CEO welcome (15 min): Why we are doing this, what success looks like, why I am excited about this team
- Consulting partner overview of approach (20 min): Here is how we work, here is what we will learn in the first 2 weeks, here is what happens after that
- Team introductions (10 min): Meet the core consulting team; they will be your main contacts
- Q&A (15 min): Address burning questions

12–1 PM: Break (let people get back to work)

1–3 PM: Core Team Kickoff
- Deep dive on objectives and hypotheses (45 min): What are we trying to figure out? What do we think we already know?
- Phase 1 scope and deliverables (45 min): What will the first 2 weeks look like? What do we need to do to move to Phase 2?
- Data and access requirements (15 min): What systems do we need? Who gives us access?
- Team working norms (15 min): Daily standup at 9 AM. Slack channel for real-time questions. Weekly team sync Friday at 4 PM.

3–4 PM: Core Team + Client Leadership Sync
- Quick recap of kickoff meeting and team responses
- Any questions or concerns
- Confirm support and resource availability

4 PM: End of day email to all employees
- Recap of what was announced
- First key deliverables: Week 1 outputs (interview summaries, initial hypotheses), Week 2 (preliminary findings, priorities for further investigation)
- Where to get more info: Intranet page, Slack channel, FAQ

**First Week (Feb 24–28)**

Daily:
- Standup at 9 AM (15 min)

Stakeholder interviews:
- 8–10 interviews conducted with customer-facing teams, operations, finance (2–3 interviews per day)
- Each interview is 60 min with 2 consulting team members + 1 client employee who knows the area well

Friday (Feb 28):
- Team sync (30 min): What are we learning? What are the early patterns?
- Retrospective (30 min): What went well? What would we do differently next week?
- Week 2 plan (30 min): Confirm next week's interview schedule and preliminary findings outline

Deliverables:
- Interview summary templates (started, one entry per interview)
- Preliminary hypothesis document (rough, < 2 pages; here is what we think we are learning)
- Interview schedule for Week 2 confirmed

**First 30 Days (Feb 24–Mar 28)**

Weeks 2–3:
- Complete customer, operations, and finance interviews (20 interviews total)
- Preliminary findings documented (patterns emerging across interviews)
- Three to four high-potential opportunities identified and prioritized
- Data gathering (if needed; e.g., cost analysis, customer data, process metrics)

Week 3:
- 30-day checkpoint (March 21): Consulting partner presents to client CEO
  - Findings: Here are the top 3 opportunities we have identified
  - Impact: Here is the potential value (revenue, cost, time, quality)
  - Recommended approach: Here is how we propose to tackle each
  - Timeline and resource plan: Here is what Phase 2 looks like (duration, team, cost)

Week 4:
- Proceed to Phase 2 (detailed design) or course-correct if needed
- Broaden team if Phase 2 scope is larger
- Communicate findings and next steps to the wider organization

**Common Mistakes to Avoid**

**Over-Planning Day 1 Details While Ignoring the First 30 Days**
Consultants will spend weeks getting the 4-hour kickoff perfect and then have no plan for what happens on Day 2. Plan Day 1, but spend even more time planning the first 30 days.

**Not Identifying Quick Wins**
Do not let the first 30 days go by without delivering something visible. Even a small win (a new dashboard, a ranked opportunity list, a redesigned process) signals momentum.

**Failing to Establish Communication Cadence Early**
If you do not establish a weekly sync, a daily standup, or regular steering committee reviews in the first week, you will never establish them. People will drift back to their day jobs. The program becomes a side project, not a priority.

**Not Building Relationships Early**
If the core team does not meet with key stakeholders in the first 30 days, resistance will harden. Meet them early. Listen. Address their concerns before they become deal-breakers.

## Combining Frameworks

These 8 frameworks are not used in isolation. They work together in a predictable sequence.

### Typical Workflow 1: Diagnosis to Accountability

1. Use **Five Whys** to drill past the symptom and find the root cause
2. Once you understand the root cause, use **2x2 Matrix** to prioritize which root causes to address first (and in what sequence)
3. Use **Crawl-Walk-Run** to phase your response to each root cause
4. Use **What-by-When** to assign accountability and deadlines for each phase
5. Use **Storyline** to structure the recommendation and present it to the steering committee
6. Use **RAG Assessment** to track progress on each workstream

Example: "Customer NPS dropped. We ran Five Whys and found three root causes: inadequate QA testing, missing product training, and weak feedback loop to product management. We prioritized them on a 2x2 (effort vs. impact). We designed a Crawl-Walk-Run for each: Crawl focuses on the highest-impact item (QA testing), with Walk and Run adding the others. We assigned What-by-When: Jane owns QA improvements by Week 2; Marcus owns training launch by Week 4; etc. We presented the full recommendation in Storyline format to the executive team. Now we are tracking progress with RAG, with a weekly steering committee update."

### Typical Workflow 2: Proposal to Execution

1. Use **Day-One Plan** to clarify what happens in the first 30 days if the project is approved
2. Use **Elevator Pitch** to summarize the recommendation in 30, 60, and 2-minute versions
3. Use **Storyline** to structure the full recommendation document or presentation
4. Complement with **Crawl-Walk-Run** to show the multi-phase roadmap
5. Use **What-by-When** for the first 90 days of execution
6. Use **RAG Assessment** for the first status update

Example: "We are proposing a $5M customer experience redesign over 18 months. We prepared an Elevator Pitch (30 sec: insights, 60 sec: recommendation, 2 min: full story). We structured the 40-page recommendation document using Storyline (Situation, Complication, Resolution). The roadmap follows Crawl-Walk-Run (pilot year 1, expand year 2, optimize year 3). If approved, here is the Day-One Plan (all-hands kickoff, core team onboarding, stakeholder meetings, and quick wins). Our first 90 days What-by-When is locked in. First monthly steering committee update will use RAG status."

### Typical Workflow 3: Leadership Communication

1. Use **Storyline** to structure the narrative (what changed, why it matters, what you recommend)
2. Support with **RAG Assessment** (here is the objective status)
3. Add **Elevator Pitch** (if interrupted, here is the 30-second version)
4. Reference **What-by-When** (here is what we committed to and what is done)

Example: "Monthly steering committee update. Slide 1 is Storyline: Situation (we are 4 weeks into the transformation), Complication (infrastructure team is 10 days behind on database migration), Resolution (we are accelerating testing by 2 weeks to stay on timeline). Slide 2 shows RAG status (4 Green projects, 2 Amber, 1 Red; all Ambers have mitigation plans). Slide 3 walks through What-by-When (completed 12/15 items on time, 2 at risk, 1 overdue by 5 days which we are addressing this week). If the CEO asks 'what is the real issue here?' I have a 30-second Elevator Pitch ready: 'Infrastructure migration is taking 50% longer than estimated because we underestimated data complexity; we are adjusting by compressing the testing phase and extending the go-live by one week.'"

These combinations are not rigid. You will adapt based on context, audience, and what the situation demands. But over time, you will see that these 8 frameworks solve most of the analytical and communication problems that come up in consulting work.
