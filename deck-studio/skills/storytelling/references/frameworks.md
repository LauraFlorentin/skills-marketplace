# Frameworks Reference

Deep-dive reference for the four core storytelling frameworks used in consulting and business presentations.

---

## 1. Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto)

### Origins
Developed by Barbara Minto at McKinsey in the 1960s. Adopted across all major consulting firms (McKinsey, BCG, Bain, Deloitte, Accenture) and investment banks. Still the gold standard for executive communication.

### Core Insight
Most people present **bottom-up**: they walk through analysis → reasoning → conclusion. This mirrors how they did the work.

Executives don't want your thought process. They want your answer. Present **top-down**: conclusion first, reasoning second, evidence third.

> "You think from the bottom up, but you present from the top down." — Barbara Minto

### Structure

```
                    [GOVERNING THOUGHT / ANSWER]
                           |
          ┌────────────────┼────────────────┐
      [KEY POINT 1]   [KEY POINT 2]   [KEY POINT 3]
          |                |                |
     [Evidence]       [Evidence]       [Evidence]
```

**Governing Thought**: The one thing you want the audience to remember. One sentence. Actionable.

**Key Points**: 3–5 supporting arguments. Must be MECE. Each should be a complete insight, not a topic.

**Evidence**: Data, case studies, benchmarks, analysis. Each piece supports the key point above it.

### Vertical vs. Horizontal Logic

**Vertical logic** (top to bottom): Each level answers the question raised by the level above it.
- "We need to cut costs" → "Why?" → "Because margins are down 8pts" → "Why?" → "Because input costs rose 23%"

**Horizontal logic** (left to right at the same level): Arguments at the same level should be:
- Inductive (all examples of the same type of thing), OR
- Deductive (A leads to B leads to C)

### MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive)

The quality check for the Pyramid's supporting arguments:

| Criterion | Meaning | Test |
|-----------|---------|------|
| Mutually Exclusive | No overlap between points | "Could I put this evidence under two different points?" |
| Collectively Exhaustive | Together they cover the full argument | "Is there an important angle I haven't covered?" |

**Bad (not MECE)**: "Revenue is down because of pricing, competition, and lower prices"
→ Pricing and lower prices overlap

**Good (MECE)**: "Revenue decline is driven by volume loss (−14%), price erosion (−6%), and mix shift to lower-margin products (−3%)"

### When to use the Pyramid Principle
- Any written recommendation or memo
- The body / "resolution" section of a consulting deck
- Individual argument slides
- Email subject lines and opening paragraphs

## 2. SCQA Framework

### Origins
An extension of the Pyramid Principle. Used to craft the *introduction* to a communication — the setup that earns the audience's attention before you deliver your pyramid.

### The Four Components

**Situation**
- Undisputed, shared context about the current state
- Should be something the audience already agrees with
- Tests: "Is this true?" → Yes. "Is this controversial?" → No.
- Example: "Our digital banking penetration is 34%, in line with peer average."

**Complication**
- The tension, change, or problem that disrupts the situation
- This is the "so what" — why anything needs to change
- Creates urgency and stakes
- Example: "However, challenger banks grew 3x faster last year and are now targeting our core demographic."

**Question**
- The implicit question the complication raises
- Usually not written on the slide — it's the question in the audience's mind
- Example (implicit): "What should we do to respond?"

**Answer**
- Your governing thought / recommendation
- This is the top of the pyramid — your headline response to the question
- Example: "We recommend a three-pillar digital acceleration strategy to retake growth momentum."

### SCQA Template
```
SITUATION: [Undisputed context — 1–2 sentences]
COMPLICATION: [The tension or problem — 1–2 sentences]
QUESTION: [Implicit — "So, what should we do?"]
ANSWER: [Your recommendation — 1 sentence, actionable]
```

### Example — Banking AI Strategy Deck

> **Situation**: Regional banks have historically competed on relationship banking and branch density.
>
> **Complication**: AI-native neobanks now offer hyper-personalized services at 40% lower cost-to-serve, and regulators are signaling lighter touch for digital-only entrants.
>
> **Question**: How should we respond before losing the next generation of customers?
>
> **Answer**: We recommend accelerating AI adoption across three fronts — personalized servicing, intelligent risk decisioning, and AI-powered cost reduction — to defend our market position and rebuild margin.

## 3. SCR Framework (Situation–Complication–Resolution)

### Relationship to SCQA
SCR is the macro structure for an entire deck. SCQA is the micro structure for the introduction.

Use **SCR** to architect the full presentation.
Use **SCQA** to write the intro / executive summary slide.
Use the **Pyramid Principle** to structure the Resolution section.

### How it maps to slides

| Section | Length | Purpose |
|---------|--------|---------|
| **Situation** | 1–3 slides | Establish shared understanding. Factual, undisputed. |
| **Complication** | 1–3 slides | Introduce the problem, opportunity, or tension. Build urgency. |
| **Resolution** | 60–80% of deck | Your recommended solution, structured by Pyramid Principle. |

### Key rule
Keep the Situation and Complication sections **short**. Executives know their own business. Don't over-explain the problem — get to the solution.

The Resolution should dominate. It's where your value is.

### Anatomy of the Resolution

```
Resolution Headline Slide: "We recommend X"
    ├── Chapter 1: First strategic pillar
    │     ├── Supporting argument slides
    │     └── Evidence / data slides
    ├── Chapter 2: Second strategic pillar
    │     └── ...
    └── Chapter 3: Third strategic pillar
          └── ...
Appendix: Supporting analysis, methodology, raw data
```

## 4. Hero's Journey (Consulting Variant)

### When to use
Use instead of SCR/Pyramid when:
- The audience is **skeptical or resistant** to the conclusion
- The recommendation requires **emotional buy-in**, not just logical agreement
- You are delivering **change** that affects the audience's identity or role
- The Pyramid Principle might feel "forced" — e.g., delivering bad news

### Structure

1. **The World Before**: Show the status quo. Build empathy for the current situation.
2. **The Call to Change**: Introduce the disruption or insight that forces a decision.
3. **The Challenge**: Acknowledge the difficulty and risk of the journey ahead. Name the obstacles.
4. **The Path**: Your recommended solution — step-by-step.
5. **The World After**: Paint a vivid picture of what success looks like.
6. **The Ask**: What you need from the audience to begin.

### Notes on use
- More narrative, less structured than Pyramid
- Useful for change management, organizational transformation, and cultural presentations
- Slower build to the recommendation — acceptable only when the audience needs to feel the problem first
- Not appropriate for time-pressed executives who want the answer in 30 seconds

## 5. "What Is / What Could Be" — Nancy Duarte's Gap Framework

### When to use
Use when the audience is **complacent, resistant to change, or doesn't yet feel urgency**. Particularly effective for organizational transformation, innovation strategy, or pitching a bold new direction.

### Core mechanics
Popularized by Nancy Duarte (author of *Resonate*), this framework leverages **gap theory** — the uncomfortable cognitive tension between current reality and an aspirational future. Once the audience feels this gap, they are intrinsically motivated to bridge it.

```
WHAT IS (Present State)          WHAT COULD BE (Future State)
─────────────────────────────    ─────────────────────────────
Current challenges               Competitive advantage achieved
Inefficiencies and risks         Growth, efficiency, scale
The status quo                   The compelling vision
          ↑                                   ↑
          └──────── THE GAP ─────────────────┘
                  (Bridge = your strategy)
```

### Steps
1. **Vividly paint "What Is"** — Current pain, missed opportunities, competitive threats. Make the audience *feel* the inadequacy of the status quo.
2. **Contrast with "What Could Be"** — A specific, tangible, aspirational vision. Not vague. Not "be more innovative." Specific future outcomes.
3. **Identify the gap** — What specifically needs to change? (Root causes, not symptoms.)
4. **The Bridge** — Your recommended strategy with a tactical roadmap to cross from current to future state.

### Key difference from SCR
SCR assumes the audience already feels the problem. Gap framework *creates* the feeling of urgency from scratch. Use Gap when you're trying to change a mindset, not just answer a question.

## 6. Amazon-Style Narrative Meetings

A distinct format pioneered by Amazon (Jeff Bezos) that replaces the classic slide presentation entirely.

### How it works
- Presenter writes a **6-page narrative memo** — full prose, structured argument, no bullet points
- First 20 minutes of the meeting: everyone reads silently
- Remaining time: high-quality debate and decision-making

### Why it matters for storytelling
This format enforces clarity that slides can hide. A wall of bullet points can mask weak thinking; a coherent 6-page prose argument cannot. The Amazon model treats good writing as the highest form of structured thinking.

### When to borrow from it
Even outside Amazon, the discipline applies:
- Write the narrative before building the deck
- If you can't write it in clear prose, your thinking isn't ready for slides
- Senior leaders should withhold their opinion until others have spoken (prevents confirmation bias)

## Framework Selection Guide

| Context | Best Framework |
|---------|---------------|
| Executive briefing / leadership update | Pyramid Principle + SCQA intro |
| Full consulting engagement deck | SCR + Pyramid for Resolution |
| Data presentation to leadership | Data Story Arc (see `data-storytelling.md`) |
| Organizational change / transformation pitch | Hero's Journey OR Gap ("What Is / What Could Be") |
| Complacent audience that doesn't feel urgency | Gap Framework (Nancy Duarte) |
| Board presentation | SCQA intro + Pyramid, maximum 10 slides |
| Client pitch / proposal | SCR + Pyramid, with strong exec summary |
| Weekly status update | SCQA compressed to 1 page |
| Email recommendation | Pyramid in paragraph form |
| High-stakes written document (no slides) | Amazon-style 6-page narrative memo |
