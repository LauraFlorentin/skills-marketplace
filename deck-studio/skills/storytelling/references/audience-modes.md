# Audience Modes Reference

How to adapt storytelling structure, tone, and depth for different business audiences.

---

## The Core Principle

**Every presentation decision — structure, length, vocabulary, level of evidence — should be driven by the audience's goal, not yours.**

Before structuring any story, answer:
1. Who is the audience?
2. What do they already know?
3. What do they care about most?
4. What do you need them to *do* after?
5. What would make them skeptical?

---

## Audience Mode 1: Executive / C-Suite

**Mindset**: Time-poor, big-picture oriented, outcome-focused. Want the recommendation before the reasoning.

**Structure**: Pyramid Principle is mandatory. SCQA intro. 1 exec summary slide.

**Length**: 8–12 slides maximum. Everything else in appendix.

**Language**: Business impact language. Revenue, cost, risk, market share. No jargon.

**Evidence**: 1–2 key data points per argument. Not the full analysis.

**Tone**: Confident, direct, decisive. Never hedge excessively.

**What they hate**:
- Long buildup before the point
- Methodology before recommendations
- Slide after slide of data with no synthesis
- Uncertainty without a recommended path forward

**Prototype opening**:
> "We have 20 minutes. My recommendation: [one sentence]. Here's why — three reasons."

---

## Audience Mode 2: Board of Directors

**Mindset**: Governance, risk, and accountability. Want to see that management has a handle on the situation.

**Structure**: SCR with heavy emphasis on the "so what" of each complication. Pyramid for resolution.

**Length**: Maximum 10 slides. Executive summary must be one page.

**Language**: Risk-adjusted, strategic, fiduciary. Connect to shareholder value.

**Evidence**: Key metrics and benchmarks. Year-over-year comparisons. Industry context.

**Tone**: Measured, credible, balanced. Acknowledge uncertainty and risk — don't paper over it.

**What they hate**:
- Surprises they weren't informed of earlier
- Defensive framing or minimizing problems
- Too much operational detail
- Lack of clear ownership for recommendations

**Prototype opening**:
> "This deck addresses three strategic decisions the board needs to make this quarter, with full analysis in the appendix."

---

## Audience Mode 3: Client (Consulting Engagement)

**Mindset**: Paying for expertise. Want to feel like you understand their business AND have a perspective they don't.

**Structure**: Full SCR deck. Strong Complication section — show you understand their problem deeply before offering solutions. MECE resolution.

**Length**: Appropriate to engagement phase. Working session: 20–30 slides. Final delivery: 40–80 slides + appendix.

**Language**: Mirror their language. Use their KPIs, their terminology, their internal framing — then add your conceptual overlay.

**Evidence**: Their data + external benchmarks. Show you've done the analysis using *their* numbers.

**Tone**: Expert but collaborative. "Here's our view" not "here's the answer."

**What they hate**:
- Generic frameworks applied without customization
- Recommendations that ignore their constraints (regulatory, organizational, financial)
- Surprise in the room — align key stakeholders before final presentation
- No actionable next steps

**Prototype opening**:
> "We've spent 6 weeks in your business. Our hypothesis going in was X. What we found was more nuanced — here's the updated picture."

---

## Audience Mode 4: Internal Stakeholders / Cross-functional Teams

**Mindset**: Busy, possibly defensive about their area. Want to know what this means for them.

**Structure**: SCQA-lite. Lead with what's changing and why it matters to them. Less formal than client decks.

**Length**: Shorter the better. 5–10 slides, or a well-written email.

**Language**: Plain language. Avoid consulting jargon in internal settings unless your audience uses it.

**Evidence**: Internal data they recognize. Quote their metrics back to them.

**Tone**: Collaborative, not prescriptive. "We'd like your input on..."

**What they hate**:
- Feeling like they're being told what to do
- Being surprised in a meeting
- Jargon that makes them feel talked-at
- Recommendations with no change management plan

---

## Audience Mode 5: Skeptical / Resistant Audience

**Mindset**: Predisposed to disagree. May have been burned before, or have competing priorities.

**Structure**: Use **Hero's Journey** variant instead of Pyramid. Build empathy before revealing the recommendation. Don't lead with the answer — they'll reject it before hearing the evidence.

**Approach**:
1. Start with their reality, their pain. Make them feel heard.
2. Walk through the data together. Let them "discover" the insight.
3. Acknowledge what you're asking them to give up or change.
4. Name the risks honestly — don't oversell.
5. Offer the recommendation as a path forward, not a dictum.

**Language**: "What we're hearing from you is..." / "The data suggests..." / "One option would be..."

**Tone**: Humble, curious, collaborative. Do not be defensive.

**What converts skeptics**:
- Being seen and acknowledged before being sold to
- Data that comes from a source they trust
- Admitting the limits of your own analysis
- A recommendation that gives them credit or agency

---

## Audience Mode 6: Mixed Audience (Various Seniority Levels)

**Challenge**: The CFO wants conclusions. The analyst team wants methodology. The ops head wants implementation detail.

**Solution**: Layer the deck.
- Executive summary slide: for the CFO
- Main body (10–15 slides): for the mixed audience
- Detailed appendix: for the analysts and ops team

Use **progressive disclosure**:
- State the conclusion in the title
- Summarize in 3 bullets in the body
- Put the full analysis in the appendix (labeled and cross-referenced)

## Mode Selection Guide

| Audience | Primary Mode | Structure | Length |
|---------|-------------|-----------|--------|
| CEO / C-Suite | Executive | Pyramid + SCQA | 8–12 slides |
| Board | Board | SCR + Pyramid | 10 slides max |
| Client (new) | Client | SCR | 30–80 slides |
| Client (status) | Client | SCQA update format | 10–15 slides |
| Internal team | Internal stakeholder | SCQA-lite | 5–10 slides |
| Skeptic | Resistant | Hero's Journey | Varies |
| Mixed audience | Layered | Exec summary + Main + Appendix | Full deck |

## Anticipating Questions

Part of great storytelling is making the audience feel like you've already thought of their question.

**Technique**: Before finalizing any deck, list the top 5 questions you'd expect from this audience. For each:
1. Does the deck already answer it?
2. If not, add it as a labeled appendix slide.
3. If it's a challenge to your recommendation, address it in the body with a "risks and mitigations" section.

**Rule**: Never let an executive ask a question in the room that you should have anticipated. It signals you don't understand their concerns.

## Language Register Cheat Sheet

| Audience | Use | Avoid |
|---------|-----|-------|
| C-Suite | ROI, shareholder value, strategic fit | "Synergies", vague frameworks, passive voice |
| Board | Risk exposure, governance, milestones | Operational minutiae, technical jargon |
| Client | Their internal KPIs and language | Your firm's internal tools/frameworks by name |
| Internal | Plain language, collaborative tone | Consulting speak, directives |
| Technical | Precise terminology, caveated claims | Oversimplification, missing nuance |

## The Strategic Use of Analogies and Metaphors

When introducing unfamiliar or complex concepts, analogies bridge the gap between the audience's existing knowledge and the new idea. Without this bridge, audiences often reject or distrust foreign concepts instinctively.

**When to use**:
- Explaining technical concepts to non-technical stakeholders
- Introducing a strategic concept with no established frame of reference
- Making abstract recommendations feel concrete and real

**Examples**:
- Cybersecurity layered protection → "home security system with perimeter, motion sensors, and a safe"
- Software development process → "construction work: blueprints before bricks"
- AI governance framework → "like a compliance function — policies, controls, and audits"

**Rules for good analogies**:
1. The analogy must be *more familiar* to the audience than the concept itself
2. Keep it in their world — use their industry, their daily life, their vocabulary
3. Don't over-extend the analogy; identify where it breaks down and acknowledge it
4. Humor and poignancy are both valid — memorable beats accurate every time

## Executive Presence (EP) as a Storytelling Multiplier

Even the best-structured deck fails if delivery undermines it. Executive Presence is the ability to inspire confidence and project authority. Per researcher Sylvia Ann Hewlett, it has three pillars:

### 1. Gravitas
- The most critical pillar: decisiveness, confidence, calm under pressure
- **The "Cheetah Pause"**: take a brief, deliberate pause before responding to a difficult question — signals evaluation and control, not flustering
- Decisive leaders use silence strategically; rushing to fill silence signals low status

### 2. Communication
- Not just what you say — tone, pacing, and delivery are equally important
- Research suggests the *tone* of delivery is more memorable than the actual words
- Avoid "nervous" verbal patterns: upward inflection at the end of statements (making assertions sound like questions), over-hedging, filler words

### 3. Image / Presence
- Non-verbal cues: use "inclusive gestures" (large, shoulder-driven movements) rather than tight/guarded postures
- Strong, distributed eye contact builds trust; avoiding eye contact signals uncertainty
- Purposeful movement and upright posture; eliminate shuffling, fidgeting, pacing

### The shifting archetype
Modern EP has moved away from the "authoritarian, commanding" archetype toward **authentic, inclusive leadership**. Today's most effective presenters:
- Listen actively before asserting
- Invite others into the conversation
- Show genuine engagement with pushback rather than defending reflexively

This shift is particularly relevant for female and minority consultants, who historically had to "perform" a narrower version of EP. Authentic leadership — aligning behavior with core values — has emerged as the most durable credibility-building strategy.

## Managing Pushback and Q&A

A presentation doesn't end at the last slide — it continues through Q&A, where the logical architecture is tested and buy-in is actually secured.

**Principles**:
- Anticipate the top 5 objections before walking in the room; prepare ghost slides for each
- Never "wing" a difficult question — if you don't know, say so and commit to a follow-up
- Thoroughness in anticipating questions builds deeper trust than a polished delivery alone
- When a senior leader is present, they should ideally withhold their view until others have spoken — prevents confirmation bias from collapsing the conversation

**Handling a direct challenge to your recommendation**:
1. Acknowledge the concern genuinely — don't be defensive
2. Confirm shared understanding: "I want to make sure I understand your concern..."
3. If the evidence supports your view: present it directly and calmly
4. If it's a legitimate alternative: acknowledge the trade-off explicitly
5. If you're wrong: say so. Credibility from intellectual honesty outlasts any single slide
