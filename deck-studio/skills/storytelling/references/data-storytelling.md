# Data Storytelling Reference

How to turn data and analysis into compelling, decision-driving narratives.

---

## The Core Problem

> "Today's corporate world is rife with poorly designed slides cluttered with inappropriate charts." — Brent Dykes

Most data presentations suffer from:
- **Over-visualization**: beautiful charts, no narrative
- **Under-contextualization**: data without meaning
- **Missing the so-what**: analysis without recommendation

Data doesn't speak for itself. Your job is to tell the audience what to *see* in the data — and what to *do* about it.

---

## The Three Components of Data Storytelling (Brent Dykes)

```
        DATA
       /    \
      /      \
NARRATIVE ── VISUALS
```

All three must work together:
- **Data** without narrative and visuals = a report nobody reads
- **Visuals** without narrative and data = decoration
- **Narrative** without data and visuals = opinion

The intersection of all three = a **data story that drives decisions**.

## The Data Story Arc

An adaptation of the classic narrative arc for data-driven business presentations.

### 1. Hook
Open with a striking data point or question that establishes stakes.

> "We are losing $2M per month — and I think I know why."

The hook should:
- Be relevant to the audience's biggest concern
- Create urgency
- Be concrete and specific (numbers beat abstractions)

### 2. Context (Setup)
Provide the background the audience needs to interpret the data.

- What is being measured, and why it matters
- What the normal / expected state looks like
- Time period and scope

> "Over the past 18 months, we've tracked acquisition cost across all 12 digital channels..."

### 3. Conflict (Tension)
Introduce the data insight that disrupts the expected narrative. This is where the story begins.

- Show what's changing, declining, underperforming, or surprising
- The audience should feel the tension: "That's worse than I expected."
- Use contrast: before vs. after, target vs. actual, us vs. benchmark

> "But in Q3, cost per acquisition spiked 34% — across every channel simultaneously."

### 4. Aha Moment (Insight)
Deliver the core insight — the thing that explains the conflict.

- Make the visualization that delivers this insight **simple and clean**
- Annotate the key data point explicitly
- This is the moment the audience goes "oh, now I see it"

> "The spike maps exactly to the date we switched attribution models — artificially inflating reported costs."

### 5. Implications
Connect the insight to business consequences. Answer "so what?"

- Dollar impact
- Strategic implications
- Risk if not addressed

### 6. Call to Action
Tell the audience exactly what you need from them.

- Decision required
- Resources needed
- Next steps and timeline

## Annotating Data Visualizations

Charts should never stand alone. Every chart needs:

1. **An action title** that states the insight (not the metric)
   - ❌ "Customer Acquisition Cost by Channel (Q1–Q3 2025)"
   - ✅ "Acquisition costs spiked 34% in Q3 — driven by attribution model change, not real cost increases"

2. **An annotation** pointing to the key data moment
   - Arrow or callout box pointing to the inflection point
   - Short explanatory text (1 sentence max)

3. **A contrast element**
   - Highlight the "hero" data in a strong color
   - Grey out everything else
   - Add a benchmark line if available ("industry avg", "target", "prior year")

## Contrast Techniques

Humans understand differences better than absolutes. Build contrast into every data story.

| Instead of | Use |
|-----------|-----|
| "Revenue is $10M" | "Revenue is $10M — 20% above target" |
| "NPS is 42" | "NPS is 42 — down from 56 six months ago" |
| "Cost-to-serve is $38" | "Our cost-to-serve is $38 vs. $22 for digital-native competitors" |

**Types of contrast**:
- **Before / After**: Show change over time
- **Us / Them**: Benchmark against competitors or peers
- **Actual / Target**: Compare performance to plan
- **Best / Worst**: Show the range and where you sit

## Zoom Technique

Move between levels of analysis to create a sense of discovery.

**Wide → Narrow** (drilling down):
> "Here's the industry trend. Here's our sector. Here's our firm. Here's the one team that's driving the issue."

**Narrow → Wide** (zooming out):
> "One customer complained. Then 10. Then 1,000. What looked like a service issue is actually a product flaw affecting our entire addressable market."

Both directions are effective. Choose based on whether you want to:
- Create specificity (wide → narrow)
- Escalate urgency (narrow → wide)

## The "Subvert Expectations" Pattern

Highly memorable data stories often use a "you might think X... but actually Y" structure.

> "You might assume our NPS decline is about pricing. But when we segment the data, price-sensitive customers are actually our most loyal. The decline is concentrated entirely in digital channels — among users who never interact with a human."

Formula:
1. State the obvious interpretation
2. Show why the data contradicts it
3. Reveal the real story
4. Show what that means

## Adapting Data Stories to Different Audiences

| Audience | Emphasis | What to include | What to remove |
|---------|----------|----------------|----------------|
| C-Suite / Board | Business impact, decision | Hook, insight, implications, ask | Methodology, raw data |
| Operations leaders | What to fix, how | Conflict, implications, call to action | Strategic context |
| Data / Analyst teams | Rigor, methodology | All components + methodology | Heavy narrative |
| General business audience | Why it matters | Context, insight, "so what" | Technical detail |

## Common Data Storytelling Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it fails | Fix |
|---------|-------------|-----|
| Showing all the data | Overwhelms, buries insight | Curate to the one number that matters |
| No chart annotation | Audience looks at the wrong thing | Add arrows, callout boxes, highlighted segments |
| Metric-only titles | Doesn't tell the audience what to think | Rewrite as an insight ("NPS declined" → "NPS declined 14pts driven by digital failures") |
| Saving the best for last | Executives stop listening | Hook with the biggest insight, then explain |
| Missing the call to action | Story entertains but doesn't drive decisions | End every data story with a specific ask |
| Correlation without causation | Damages credibility | Acknowledge limitations, use hedged language |
