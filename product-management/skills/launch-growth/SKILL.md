# Launch & Growth

## Disclaimer

Launch and growth tactics should be adapted to your specific market, product stage, and regulatory environment. Test assumptions before scaling.

---

## Go-to-Market

A go-to-market (GTM) plan answers three questions: Who are we selling to? How will they learn about us? What does the buying process look like? The complexity of the plan scales with the complexity of the product and organization. A two-person startup does not need a 40-page GTM brief. An enterprise launching into a new market does.

### Startup Mode: Lean GTM

For early-stage products, your GTM strategy is simple: find the people who have the problem most urgently, get the product in front of them, and learn as fast as possible. You do not need a marketing department. You need hustle, specificity, and speed.

#### Identify Early Adopters

Early adopters are not "everyone who might use this." They are the people whose pain is so acute that they will tolerate a buggy, incomplete product to get relief. To find them, answer these questions:

- **Who has the problem most urgently?** Look for people currently using spreadsheets, manual workarounds, or cobbled-together tool stacks to solve the problem your product addresses. They are actively investing time and effort, which means they will pay to save it.
- **Where do they already gather?** Identify 3-5 communities: Slack groups, Discord servers, subreddits, Twitter/X circles, LinkedIn groups, niche forums, industry meetups. Your first 50 users are already in these places.
- **What language do they use?** Listen to how they describe the problem. Use their words in your messaging, not your product jargon.

#### Launch Channels for Startups

**Product Hunt launch playbook:**
1. Build a "hunter" relationship 2-4 weeks before launch. Reach out to top hunters in your category.
2. Prepare assets: tagline (under 60 characters), description (under 260 characters), 4-6 images/GIFs showing the product, a 1-minute demo video, a maker comment with your backstory.
3. Launch on a Tuesday or Wednesday. Avoid Mondays (competition from weekend builders) and Fridays (lower traffic).
4. Have your team, advisors, and early users ready to upvote and comment in the first 2 hours. Authentic comments matter more than vote count.
5. Respond to every comment personally. Engage for the full 24-hour cycle.

**Hacker News (Show HN) playbook:**
1. Title format: "Show HN: [Product Name] -- [One sentence describing what it does]."
2. Lead with the technical or design insight, not the marketing pitch. HN rewards substance over hype.
3. Include a text body explaining: what problem it solves, how it works (technical details appreciated), what you learned building it, what feedback you want.
4. Post between 8-10 AM ET on weekdays. Avoid weekends.
5. Respond to every comment thoughtfully. Engage with criticism gracefully -- it builds credibility.

**Beta waitlist strategy:**
1. Create a landing page with a clear value proposition, one email capture field, and a short explainer (video or 3 bullet points).
2. Add a referral mechanic: "Move up the waitlist by sharing with friends." Tools like Viral Loops or custom referral codes work.
3. Send a welcome email immediately on signup. Include expected timeline and what makes this product different.
4. Email the waitlist weekly with progress updates. Build anticipation and trust.
5. Invite in small batches (10-25 at a time). This lets you manage support load and iterate between batches.

**Founder-led sales (the first 10 customers):**
Your first 10 customers should not come from inbound marketing. They should come from you personally reaching out to people who fit your ideal customer profile. This is not scalable. It is not supposed to be. The goal is to learn, not to grow.
1. Identify 50 potential customers by name. Use LinkedIn, community directories, conference attendee lists.
2. Send personalized outreach (not templates). Reference something specific about their work or pain point.
3. Offer a 30-minute call to understand their workflow. Do not pitch. Listen.
4. After the call, offer free access to the product in exchange for 2 feedback sessions over the next month.
5. Close the loop: Did the product solve their problem? What is missing? Would they pay? How much?

**Community seeding:**
Find 3-5 communities where your target users already spend time. Contribute value before promoting anything. Answer questions. Share insights. Build a reputation. Only after you have established credibility should you mention your product -- and even then, frame it as "I built something to solve this problem" rather than "check out my product."

**Content marketing basics:**
- Write 2-3 blog posts that address the specific pain points your product solves. Optimize for the search terms your customers use.
- Create Twitter/X threads that break down industry problems, share data, or tell your building-in-public story.
- Write a "Show HN" or "Indie Hackers" post about your journey, lessons, or technical decisions.

### Enterprise Mode: Cross-Functional GTM

For established products launching new capabilities, entering new markets, or releasing major versions, GTM requires coordination across sales, marketing, customer success, product, and sometimes legal and compliance. The risk is not obscurity -- it is misalignment.

#### Sales Enablement

Sales teams need four things to sell effectively:

**Battle cards (one per major competitor):**

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| **Competitor overview** | Who they are, what they sell, pricing, target market |
| **Where we win** | 3-5 specific advantages with proof points (customer quotes, benchmark data, feature comparisons) |
| **Where they win** | 2-3 areas where the competitor is stronger. Honest assessment -- sales reps will be asked about these. Equip them with responses, not denial. |
| **Objection handling matrix** | Top 5 objections buyers raise when comparing, with scripted responses |
| **Demo talking points** | Which features to highlight, which to avoid, recommended demo flow for competitive deals |
| **Pricing talk track** | How to position your pricing vs. the competitor's. When to discount (rarely), when to hold firm, how to reframe value. |

**Objection handling matrix template:**

| Objection | Why They Say It | Response | Proof Point |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Competitor X is cheaper" | They are comparing sticker price, not TCO | "When you factor in implementation, maintenance, and the integrations you will need to add, the total cost over 2 years is actually 20% lower with us. Here is a TCO comparison..." | [Customer name] saved $45K/yr after switching from Competitor X |
| "We already use Competitor Y" | Switching cost concern | "We integrate with Y and most customers run both during a 90-day transition. Here is a migration guide..." | Average migration time: 3 weeks with zero downtime |
| "You do not have Feature Z" | Feature gap, real or perceived | "We approach that differently. Instead of Z, we offer [alternative] which achieves the same outcome with less configuration..." | 85% of customers who asked about Z found our approach sufficient |

**Demo scripts:** Structure every demo as Problem, Solution, Proof. Open with the customer's stated pain point (from the discovery call), show the product solving it (live, not slides), close with a proof point (customer story, metric, case study). Demos should be under 20 minutes. Save 10 minutes for questions.

**Pricing talk tracks:** Train sales on value framing: "The question is not whether $X/month is expensive. The question is whether saving Y hours per week and reducing Z% errors is worth $X/month to your team." Provide a calculator that quantifies ROI based on the customer's inputs.

#### Partner Channel Activation

If you sell through partners (resellers, system integrators, technology partners):

1. **Partner onboarding:** Provide partners with product training, demo environments, and sales materials 2-4 weeks before GA.
2. **Co-marketing:** Joint webinars, co-branded case studies, partner blog posts. Agree on messaging and approval process upfront.
3. **Deal registration:** Ensure the partner portal is updated with the new product/SKU. Clarify margin structure and discount authority.
4. **Technical certification:** If partners implement or support the product, require certification before they can sell or support it.

### Pricing Strategy Basics

Pricing is the most underleveraged growth lever. A 1% improvement in pricing yields a larger profit impact than a 1% improvement in customer acquisition or cost reduction. Yet most PMs spend less than an hour on pricing decisions.

#### Pricing Approaches

**Value-based pricing:** Price based on the value the customer receives, not what it costs you to deliver. To determine value: quantify the customer's current cost of the problem (time, money, risk), then price your solution as a fraction of that savings. If your product saves a team $100K/year, pricing at $20K/year is easy to justify. This is the recommended approach for software products.

**Competitor-based pricing:** Set your price relative to the market rate, adjusted for positioning. If you offer more value, price 10-20% above. If you are the challenger with less brand recognition, price 10-30% below. Use this when the market has established price anchors that customers use as reference points.

**Cost-plus pricing:** Calculate your cost to deliver (COGS, support, infrastructure) and add a margin. Appropriate for hardware, professional services, or commoditized products where the customer is price-shopping. Not recommended for software -- your value to the customer is typically far above your delivery cost.

#### Freemium vs. Free Trial vs. Paid-Only

| Criteria | Freemium | Free Trial | Paid-Only |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best when** | Product has viral/network effects; large addressable market; low marginal cost per user | Product value is clear but takes time to experience; complex setup | High ACV; small addressable market; requires human onboarding |
| **CAC tolerance** | Low (self-serve acquisition) | Medium (some sales touch) | High (sales-led) |
| **Product complexity** | Low (user can get value without training) | Medium (guided setup acceptable) | High (requires implementation or consultation) |
| **Time-to-value** | Instant or within first session | Within trial period (7-30 days) | Demonstrated during sales process |
| **Market maturity** | Growing market (land-grab phase) | Established market (conversion optimization phase) | Mature market (relationship-driven sales) |
| **Conversion benchmark** | 2-5% free-to-paid | 10-25% trial-to-paid | N/A (100% paid from day 1) |
| **Risk** | Supporting large free user base that never converts | Users not activating during trial window | Missing out on word-of-mouth and organic discovery |

**Decision rule:** If your product has a clear "aha moment" that users can reach independently within 5 minutes, freemium works. If the aha moment takes days or requires configuration, free trial is better. If the aha moment requires a custom demo or human walkthrough, go paid-only with a strong sales process.

#### Pricing Page Best Practices

1. **Show 3 tiers.** The middle tier should be your target -- most customers anchor on the center option. Name tiers by customer segment (Starter, Professional, Enterprise), not by feature list.
2. **Highlight the recommended plan.** Use visual emphasis (color, badge, border) on the tier you want most customers to choose.
3. **Annual vs. monthly toggle.** Show the annual price by default with the monthly savings percentage. Annual billing improves cash flow and retention.
4. **Feature comparison table below the tiers.** Let customers self-serve the comparison. Bold the differentiating features between tiers.
5. **Include social proof.** Logos of recognizable customers, a testimonial, or "Trusted by X teams" near the pricing.
6. **Enterprise tier should say "Contact Sales."** Do not show enterprise pricing publicly. This allows value-based negotiation and prevents anchoring.
7. **Address objections on the page.** FAQ section: "Can I switch plans?" "What happens if I cancel?" "Is there a setup fee?" "Do you offer discounts for nonprofits/education?"

## Launch Management

A launch is a coordination event. The product work is done -- now you need to ensure that the right people know, the right systems are ready, and the right processes are in place to handle what comes next. A botched launch wastes months of product and engineering effort.

### Startup Mode: Simple Launch Checklist

For small teams shipping frequently, you do not need a 50-step process. You need a short, reliable checklist that catches the basics every time. Pin this in your team channel and run through it before every release.

```
STARTUP LAUNCH CHECKLIST
==========================
Feature / Release: _______________
Ship date: _______________
Owner: _______________

PRE-LAUNCH:
[ ] Feature complete -- all acceptance criteria met
[ ] Basic QA pass -- happy path works, edge cases documented
[ ] Landing page / in-app announcement updated
[ ] Analytics instrumented -- key events tracked:
    [ ] Signup / registration
    [ ] Activation event (first value moment)
    [ ] Core action (the thing users came to do)
    [ ] Upgrade / payment (if applicable)
    [ ] Error events
[ ] Support email ready (support@, or founder's inbox)
[ ] Social media posts drafted (Twitter/X, LinkedIn, relevant communities)
[ ] Changelog or release notes written
[ ] Founder / on-call person available for day-1 support

LAUNCH DAY:
[ ] Deploy to production
[ ] Smoke test in production (manual check of critical paths)
[ ] Social media posts published
[ ] Community posts published (if applicable)
[ ] Team notified in Slack / chat
[ ] Monitoring dashboards checked (errors, latency)

POST-LAUNCH (24-48 hours):
[ ] Review analytics -- are events firing correctly?
[ ] Respond to all user feedback within 4 hours
[ ] Fix any critical bugs within 24 hours
[ ] Share initial results with team (signups, activation, feedback)
[ ] Document what went well and what to improve for next launch
```

### Enterprise Mode: Phased Rollout

For products with large user bases, enterprise customers, SLAs, or complex dependencies, launching to 100% of users on day one is reckless. Use a phased rollout to manage risk, collect data, and build confidence incrementally.

#### Rollout Phases

| Phase | Duration | Audience | Purpose | Exit Criteria |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1: Internal Dogfood** | 1 week | Internal team (company employees) | Catch obvious bugs, validate core flows, test monitoring | No P1/P2 bugs. Core flows work end-to-end. Monitoring dashboards confirmed working. |
| **Phase 2: Beta Users** | 2-4 weeks | 5-10% of users (opt-in beta cohort or selected accounts) | Validate with real users at small scale. Collect qualitative feedback. Monitor performance metrics. | Error rate within 1.5x baseline. No new P1 bugs. Positive qualitative feedback from 70%+ of beta users. |
| **Phase 3: Controlled Rollout** | 2 weeks | 25% then 50% of users | Validate at scale. Load test infrastructure. Monitor funnel metrics against baseline. | Error rate within 1.2x baseline. Latency within 1.5x baseline. Funnel metrics stable or improving. Support ticket volume manageable. |
| **Phase 4: General Availability** | -- | 100% of users | Full launch with all communication and enablement | All prior exit criteria sustained for 48 hours at 50%. |

**Between each phase:** Hold a 30-minute go/no-go meeting. Review the exit criteria. If any criterion is not met, either fix the issue before proceeding or roll back. The decision to proceed is explicit, not assumed.

#### Feature Flags Strategy

Feature flags are the mechanism that makes phased rollouts possible. They decouple deployment from release -- code ships to production but is only visible to targeted users.

**Flag naming convention:** `[team].[feature].[variant]` -- for example, `growth.new-onboarding.v2` or `checkout.one-click-purchase.enabled`. Consistent naming prevents confusion when managing dozens of flags.

**Flag lifecycle:**
1. **Created:** Flag added to code and flag management system. Default state: off.
2. **Testing:** Flag enabled for internal users and QA environments.
3. **Beta:** Flag enabled for beta cohort (percentage or user list).
4. **Rollout:** Flag gradually increased from 25% to 50% to 100%.
5. **GA (permanent on):** Flag set to 100%. Code path for "flag off" is now dead code.
6. **Cleanup:** Remove the flag, the conditional logic, and the "off" code path. This is the step most teams skip, and it leads to thousands of stale flags cluttering the codebase.

**Cleanup rule:** Every flag must have a cleanup date set at creation. Default: 30 days after reaching 100%. Assign an owner. Track stale flags in a monthly hygiene review.

#### Rollback Plan

Define rollback triggers before launch, not during an incident. When any of these conditions are met, roll back immediately (disable the feature flag) and investigate:

| Trigger | Threshold | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Error rate | >2x baseline for 15+ minutes | Roll back immediately. Page on-call engineer. |
| Latency (p95) | >3x baseline for 15+ minutes | Roll back immediately. Investigate infrastructure. |
| Critical bug (P1) | Any user-facing data loss, security issue, or complete workflow breakage | Roll back immediately. Incident response activated. |
| Support ticket spike | >3x daily average within 4 hours | Pause rollout (do not expand). Investigate ticket themes. Roll back if tickets indicate fundamental UX or data issue. |
| Funnel metric degradation | Core metric (activation, conversion) drops >10% vs. control | Pause rollout. Analyze by segment. Roll back if degradation persists after 48 hours. |

**Rollback process:**
1. Disable the feature flag (returns all users to the previous experience).
2. Notify stakeholders: engineering, product, CS, sales (if customer-facing).
3. Post in the incident channel with: what happened, when the rollback occurred, who is investigating, and estimated timeline for next update.
4. Do not re-enable the flag until the root cause is identified, fixed, and verified.

#### Monitoring Dashboards

Set up dashboards before launch. You should not be building monitoring on launch day.

**Dashboard 1: System Health**
- Error rate (5xx errors as % of requests) with pre-launch baseline marked
- Latency (p50, p95, p99) with pre-launch baseline marked
- Infrastructure metrics (CPU, memory, queue depth) if relevant
- Deployment markers (when code was deployed, when flags were changed)

**Dashboard 2: Product Metrics**
- Funnel metrics: signup, activation, core action, conversion (compare flag-on vs. flag-off cohorts)
- Feature adoption: how many users are engaging with the new feature, session duration, completion rate
- Retention: early signal (Day 1, Day 7 retention for new cohort vs. historical)

**Dashboard 3: Support and Sentiment**
- Support ticket volume (total and categorized by topic)
- Ticket themes (top issues, new issue categories)
- NPS or in-app feedback scores (if instrumented)

#### Incident Response

| Severity | Definition | Response Time | Owner | Communication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P1 - Critical** | Service down, data loss, security breach, or >50% of users affected | 15 minutes | On-call engineer + engineering lead | All-hands page. Status page updated. Customer comms within 1 hour. |
| **P2 - Major** | Significant feature broken, >10% of users affected, or SLA at risk | 30 minutes | On-call engineer | Team Slack channel. Status page updated if customer-facing. |
| **P3 - Minor** | Non-critical bug, cosmetic issue, or workaround available | 4 hours | Assigned engineer | Tracked in bug tracker. No external communication needed. |
| **P4 - Low** | Minor inconvenience, polish issue, or edge case | Next sprint | Backlog | Tracked in bug tracker. |

**Escalation path:** P4 -> P3: Engineering manager. P3 -> P2: Engineering lead + PM. P2 -> P1: VP Engineering + VP Product + Customer comms team.

## A/B Testing

A/B testing replaces opinions with evidence. It is the primary mechanism for making product decisions when you have enough traffic to measure outcomes statistically. Done well, it compounds: each test teaches you something that makes the next test smarter. Done poorly, it gives you false confidence in bad decisions.

### Hypothesis Format

Every test starts with a hypothesis. A hypothesis is not "let's try changing the button color." A hypothesis is a falsifiable prediction that connects a change to a measurable outcome.

**Format:**

> "We believe **[change]** will cause **[effect]** for **[segment]**, which we will measure by **[metric]** over **[timeframe]**."

**Worked examples:**

1. > "We believe **replacing the 5-field signup form with a single-field email capture** will cause **a 15% increase in signup completion rate** for **new visitors from paid ads**, which we will measure by **signup completion rate** over **2 weeks**."

2. > "We believe **adding a progress bar to the onboarding flow** will cause **a 10% increase in onboarding completion** for **users who start onboarding**, which we will measure by **onboarding completion rate (step 1 to step 5)** over **3 weeks**."

3. > "We believe **showing personalized product recommendations on the dashboard** will cause **a 5% increase in second-session return rate** for **activated free users**, which we will measure by **Day-7 retention rate** over **4 weeks**."

### Test Design

#### Control vs. Variant

- **Control (A):** The current experience. No changes. This is your baseline.
- **Variant (B):** The changed experience. Change only one thing at a time. If you change the button color, copy, and placement simultaneously, you will not know which change drove the result.

#### Sample Size Calculation

You need enough users in each group to detect a meaningful difference with statistical confidence. The three inputs:

- **Significance level (alpha):** 95% confidence (alpha = 0.05) is the standard. This means a 5% chance of a false positive -- declaring a winner when there is no real difference.
- **Statistical power:** 80% is the standard (beta = 0.20). This means a 20% chance of a false negative -- missing a real difference.
- **Minimum detectable effect (MDE):** The smallest improvement you care about. If your current conversion rate is 10%, an MDE of 1 percentage point (10% to 11%) requires a much larger sample than an MDE of 3 percentage points (10% to 13%).

**Quick estimation formula** (for conversion rate tests):

```
Sample size per variant = 16 * (conversion rate) * (1 - conversion rate) / (MDE)^2
```

**Example:** Current conversion rate = 10% (0.10). MDE = 2 percentage points (0.02).

```
N = 16 * 0.10 * 0.90 / (0.02)^2
N = 16 * 0.09 / 0.0004
N = 1.44 / 0.0004
N = 3,600 per variant (7,200 total)
```

#### Test Duration

Minimum test duration = sample size per variant / (daily traffic to the test * traffic allocation per variant).

**Example:** You need 3,600 users per variant. Your page gets 1,000 visitors per day. You are splitting 50/50.

```
Duration = 3,600 / (1,000 * 0.50) = 7.2 days
```

Round up. Run for a minimum of 8 days. Always run tests for at least 1 full week to account for day-of-week effects (weekday vs. weekend behavior can differ significantly).

#### Traffic Allocation

- **Default: 50/50.** Maximizes statistical power and minimizes test duration.
- **Use smaller variant allocation (e.g., 90/10 or 80/20) when:** The change is risky (could break things), the feature is revenue-critical, or you want to limit exposure while testing. This increases the time needed to reach significance but reduces risk.
- **Never allocate less than 5% to a variant.** Below that threshold, you will never accumulate enough data to reach significance in a reasonable timeframe.

### Startup Mode: Quick Validation Tests

When you have limited traffic or need to validate demand before building, use lightweight testing methods that generate signal without requiring statistical sample sizes.

| Method | What It Is | Effort | Signal Quality | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fake door test** | Add a button or link for a feature that does not exist yet. When users click, show a "coming soon" message and capture their email. Measure click-through rate. | Low (1-2 hours) | Medium (measures interest, not willingness to use) | Validating demand before building |
| **Painted door test** | Show a preview or mockup of a feature (screenshot, description). Measure engagement with the preview (clicks, sign-ups, time on page). | Low (2-4 hours) | Medium (measures interest in the concept) | Validating feature concepts with existing users |
| **Wizard of Oz** | Present a fully functional-looking interface, but fulfill requests manually behind the scenes. The user does not know it is manual. | Medium (days) | High (measures real usage behavior) | Validating complex features before automating |
| **Concierge MVP** | Do the job manually for the first users -- no software, just you providing the service. | Medium (ongoing time) | High (measures willingness to pay and real value) | Validating the core value proposition |

**When to use each:**
- Use **fake door** when you need a quick demand signal and cannot afford to build anything.
- Use **painted door** when you want to compare interest across multiple feature concepts.
- Use **Wizard of Oz** when the feature requires complex logic but you want to test the user experience first.
- Use **concierge** when you need to deeply understand the problem and are willing to invest time per user.

### Enterprise Mode: Structured Experimentation

At scale, testing is a discipline, not an ad hoc activity. It requires infrastructure, governance, and statistical rigor.

#### Multi-Variant Tests (A/B/C/n)

Test multiple variants simultaneously. Useful when you have several hypotheses about the best approach. Caution: each additional variant increases the sample size needed, because you must correct for multiple comparisons (see pitfalls below).

**Sample size per variant increases roughly linearly with the number of variants.** A 3-variant test (A/B/C) needs approximately 50% more total traffic than a 2-variant test (A/B) to achieve the same power.

#### Holdout Groups

Reserve 10% of users who never see new changes. This long-term control group lets you measure the cumulative impact of all changes over time. Without a holdout, you cannot answer: "Are all these incremental optimizations actually making the product better overall?"

**Holdout group rules:**
- Size: 5-10% of total user base.
- Duration: 6-12 months (reset annually).
- Measurement: Compare holdout group metrics (retention, revenue, engagement) to the main population quarterly.
- Caution: Holdout users get a worse experience over time. This is an ethical trade-off. The organizational value of knowing the cumulative impact justifies the cost if the holdout is small and temporary.

#### Sequential Testing

Traditional tests require a fixed sample size determined upfront. Sequential testing allows you to check results at intervals and stop early if the result is already significant. This saves time and traffic.

**When to use:** High-traffic environments where waiting for the full sample size is unnecessarily slow. The variant is clearly winning or losing early.

**How it works:** Define analysis checkpoints (e.g., every 1,000 users). At each checkpoint, apply a sequential testing correction (e.g., alpha spending function) that adjusts the significance threshold to account for repeated looks. Use tools like Optimizely, Eppo, or Statsig that implement this natively.

#### Bayesian vs. Frequentist Approaches

| Aspect | Frequentist | Bayesian |
|---|---|---|
| **Question answered** | "Is the difference statistically significant?" | "What is the probability that B is better than A?" |
| **Output** | p-value (reject or fail to reject null hypothesis) | Probability distribution of the effect size |
| **Sample size** | Must be determined upfront | Can update beliefs as data accumulates |
| **Interpretability** | Harder to explain to non-technical stakeholders | Easier: "There is a 94% chance variant B is better" |
| **Best for** | High-stakes decisions requiring rigorous evidence (e.g., pricing changes, major UX overhauls) | Iterative optimization where speed matters more than certainty (e.g., headline testing, layout tweaks) |

**Practical guidance:** Use frequentist methods for tests that will change pricing, remove features, or affect revenue directly. Use Bayesian methods for rapid iteration tests where you need directional signal quickly.

#### Guardrail Metrics

Every test has a primary metric (the thing you are trying to improve) and guardrail metrics (the things that must not get worse). Define guardrails before the test starts.

**Common guardrail metrics:**

| Guardrail | Threshold | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| Page load time (p95) | Must not increase >200ms | Slow pages hurt all downstream metrics |
| Error rate (5xx) | Must not increase >0.5% | Broken features erode trust |
| Support ticket volume | Must not increase >2x | UX confusion creates support burden |
| Revenue per user | Must not decrease >2% | Conversion optimization must not reduce monetization |
| Bounce rate | Must not increase >5% | Changes should not drive users away |

If a test wins on the primary metric but fails a guardrail, do not ship it. Fix the guardrail issue first, then re-test.

### Common A/B Testing Pitfalls

These mistakes are pervasive. Every product team encounters them. Knowing about them is not enough -- you need processes that prevent them.

**Peeking (checking results too early):** Looking at results before reaching the required sample size inflates your false positive rate. If you check a test daily for 14 days, your effective false positive rate can rise from 5% to 25-40%. Prevention: Set the test duration upfront and do not make decisions until it is reached. Use sequential testing if you need to check early.

**Underpowered tests:** Running tests without enough traffic to detect a meaningful difference. The test runs for weeks, never reaches significance, and you either give up or declare "no difference" -- but the test simply could not detect a difference. Prevention: Calculate sample size before starting. If you do not have enough traffic, do not run the test -- use qualitative methods instead.

**Multiple comparison problem:** Testing 5 variants and declaring the winner based on the best-performing one. With 5 comparisons at alpha = 0.05, your chance of at least one false positive is ~23%. Prevention: Apply Bonferroni correction (divide alpha by the number of comparisons) or use a method designed for multiple comparisons.

**Novelty effects:** New features often get a usage spike simply because they are new. This spike fades. If you measure too early, you mistake temporary curiosity for sustained improvement. Prevention: Run tests for at least 2 full weeks. Compare week-1 vs. week-2 metrics within the variant. If week-2 drops significantly, the effect may not be durable.

**Not segmenting results:** The overall result is flat, but the variant is a big win for mobile users and a big loss for desktop users. These cancel out in aggregate. Prevention: Always segment results by device, user type (new vs. returning), geography, and any other relevant dimension. Look for heterogeneous treatment effects.

**HiPPO decisions (Highest Paid Person's Opinion):** The test shows variant A wins, but the VP prefers variant B. The team ships variant B. This destroys testing culture. Prevention: Agree on the decision rule before the test starts. Document it in the test plan. "If variant B achieves a statistically significant improvement of >X% on [metric], we will ship it." Make the decision rule the authority, not the executive.

## Growth Experiments

Growth is not about tactics -- it is about systematically identifying the highest-leverage changes to your funnel and running experiments to validate them. Growth teams that succeed share three traits: they measure everything, they experiment constantly, and they learn from failures as rigorously as from successes.

### Pirate Metrics (AARRR)

The AARRR framework (Dave McClure) breaks the customer journey into five stages. Every product should be able to identify its key metric for each stage and know which stage is the current bottleneck.

```
AARRR FUNNEL
=============

  ACQUISITION        How users find you
  |                  Metrics: visits, signups, app installs, channel CAC
  v
  ACTIVATION         First "aha moment" -- user gets value
  |                  Metrics: onboarding completion, first key action,
  v                  time-to-value
  RETENTION          Users come back repeatedly
  |                  Metrics: DAU/MAU, D7/D30 retention, session
  v                  frequency
  REVENUE            Users pay (or generate revenue)
  |                  Metrics: conversion rate, ARPU, LTV, MRR
  v
  REFERRAL           Users bring other users
                     Metrics: invite rate, viral coefficient (K-factor),
                     referral conversion rate
```

**For each stage, define:**
1. The specific metric you track (e.g., "Day-7 retention" not just "retention").
2. The current baseline value.
3. The target value.
4. The biggest drop-off point within that stage.

**Where to focus:** Find the stage with the biggest drop-off. If 10,000 users visit your site but only 500 sign up (5% acquisition-to-activation), fixing that gap has more impact than optimizing referral. Work on the leakiest part of the funnel first.

### Growth Loop Types

Growth loops are self-reinforcing cycles where the output of one step feeds the input of the next. Unlike linear funnels, loops compound over time.

**Viral loops:** User invites friend -> Friend signs up -> Friend invites their friends -> Loop continues.
- Driven by product mechanics: sharing, collaboration, invitations.
- Works best when the product is more valuable with more users (network effects) or when sharing is a natural part of the workflow (e.g., sending a document, sharing a playlist).
- Key metric: Viral coefficient (K-factor) = invites sent per user * conversion rate per invite. K > 1 means organic growth without paid acquisition.
- Example: Dropbox's referral program (invite a friend, both get extra storage).

**Content loops:** Users create content -> Content is indexed by search engines -> New users discover content via search -> New users create more content.
- Driven by SEO and user-generated content.
- Works best for platforms with publicly accessible content (marketplaces, forums, review sites, tools that generate shareable outputs).
- Key metric: Organic search traffic growth rate, content-to-signup conversion rate.
- Example: Pinterest (users pin content -> pins rank in Google Images -> new users discover Pinterest).

**Paid loops:** Users generate revenue -> Revenue funds ads -> Ads acquire new users -> New users generate revenue.
- Driven by efficient unit economics: LTV > CAC with enough margin to reinvest.
- Works best when you have predictable CAC and LTV, typically after product-market fit.
- Key metric: LTV:CAC ratio (target >3:1 for healthy paid loops), payback period.
- Example: Subscription SaaS with known CAC and 12-month LTV that reinvests revenue into paid channels.

**Sales loops:** Revenue funds sales team -> Sales team closes new customers -> New customers generate revenue -> Revenue funds more sales hires.
- Driven by high ACV deals that justify human sales effort.
- Works best for enterprise products with long sales cycles and large deal sizes.
- Key metric: Sales efficiency (net new ARR / fully loaded sales cost). Target >1.0.
- Example: Enterprise SaaS with $50K+ ACV that can afford dedicated account executives.

### ICE Scoring for Experiment Prioritization

When you have more experiment ideas than capacity to run them, prioritize with ICE scoring. It is fast, transparent, and good enough to focus effort on the right experiments.

**Score each experiment 1-10 on three dimensions:**

| Dimension | Question | 1 (Low) | 5 (Medium) | 10 (High) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Impact** | If this works, how big is the effect on our target metric? | Marginal improvement (<1%) | Moderate improvement (5-10%) | Transformative improvement (>20%) |
| **Confidence** | How sure are we that this will work? (Based on data, analogies, or past tests) | Pure guess, no supporting data | Some data or analogy from similar products | Strong data: user research, past test results, or proven pattern |
| **Ease** | How quickly and cheaply can we run this test? | >2 weeks of engineering + design | 3-5 days of work | <1 day, copy change or config change |

**ICE Score = Impact * Confidence * Ease**

**Example prioritization:**

| Experiment | Impact | Confidence | Ease | ICE Score | Rank |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Simplify signup form | 7 | 8 | 9 | 504 | 1 |
| Add social proof to pricing page | 6 | 6 | 8 | 288 | 2 |
| Redesign onboarding flow | 9 | 5 | 3 | 135 | 3 |
| Build referral program | 8 | 4 | 2 | 64 | 4 |

Run experiments in ICE-score order. Review and re-score monthly as you learn.

**ICE limitations:** Scores are subjective. Different people will score the same experiment differently. Mitigate this by: (1) having the team score independently, then averaging, and (2) calibrating by reviewing past experiment scores vs. actual outcomes. Over time, the team gets better at estimating.

### Startup Mode: High-Tempo Testing

Startups do not have the traffic for statistical significance on most tests. That is fine. The goal at this stage is learning speed, not statistical precision.

**Principles:**
- **Run 1-2 experiments per week.** If you are running fewer, you are not learning fast enough. If you are running more, you are probably not measuring properly.
- **Qualitative signals are valid.** Five user conversations that all point in the same direction are more actionable than a statistically insignificant A/B test with 200 users. Talk to users constantly.
- **Fail fast and move on.** If an experiment does not show a clear signal within 1-2 weeks, kill it and try the next idea. Do not let experiments linger.
- **Document every experiment and its outcome.** Build an experiment log. After 3 months, you will have 20-30 experiments. Patterns will emerge that inform your strategy.
- **Time-box, do not sample-size.** Set a calendar deadline (e.g., 1 week), not a sample size target. At the deadline, evaluate whatever data you have plus qualitative feedback, then decide.

**Startup experiment log template:**

### Enterprise Mode: Growth Team Structure and Governance

At scale, growth is a team sport with dedicated resources and formal processes.

#### Growth Team Structure

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| **Growth PM** | Owns the experiment roadmap. Prioritizes experiments using ICE or similar framework. Writes hypotheses and success criteria. Ensures experiments run to completion. |
| **Growth Engineer** | Implements experiments. Builds and maintains experimentation infrastructure (feature flags, event tracking, data pipelines). Ensures experiment code is clean and removable. |
| **Data Analyst** | Designs experiments statistically (sample size, duration, segmentation). Analyzes results. Identifies segments and patterns. Maintains the experiment results database. |
| **Growth Designer** | Designs variant experiences. Ensures experiments do not degrade UX for the sake of metrics. Advocates for the user in experiment design. |

**Reporting line:** Growth teams can report to Product, Marketing, or directly to the CEO. The key is that they have autonomy to run experiments across the product without being blocked by feature teams. Cross-functional authority is essential.

#### Experiment Review Board

Hold a weekly 30-minute meeting with the growth team, engineering lead, and PM lead:

**Agenda:**
1. **Results review (15 min):** Review completed experiments. For each: hypothesis, result (win/loss/inconclusive), key metrics, what we learned, next step (ship, kill, iterate).
2. **New experiment approval (10 min):** Review proposed experiments for the next week. Check: hypothesis is well-formed, success criteria are clear, guardrails are defined, there are no conflicts with other running experiments.
3. **Pipeline check (5 min):** Are we maintaining the target experiment velocity? What is blocking upcoming experiments?

**Decision rules:**
- **Ship:** Primary metric improved significantly, no guardrail violations.
- **Kill:** Primary metric did not improve or guardrails were violated.
- **Iterate:** Results are promising but inconclusive. Design a follow-up experiment with refined hypothesis.
- **Escalate:** Results conflict with strategic direction or the experiment revealed an unexpected finding. Bring to PM + leadership for discussion.

#### Statistical Rigor Requirements

| Requirement | Standard |
|---|---|
| Minimum significance level | 95% (alpha = 0.05) |
| Minimum power | 80% (beta = 0.20) |
| Minimum test duration | 1 full business cycle (typically 1 week minimum) |
| Guardrail metrics defined | Required for every experiment |
| Pre-registered hypothesis | Required (no post-hoc hypothesis hunting) |
| Segmentation analysis | Required for all experiments (device, user type, geography at minimum) |
| Novelty effect check | Required for UX changes (compare week 1 vs. week 2 within variant) |

#### Portfolio Approach

Not all experiments are created equal. Maintain a balanced portfolio:

| Category | Description | % of Experiments | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Quick wins** | Small, low-risk changes with high confidence. Copy tweaks, button colors, layout adjustments. | 60% | Change CTA from "Start free trial" to "Try free for 14 days" |
| **Medium bets** | Moderate effort, moderate confidence. New feature variants, onboarding flow changes, pricing experiments. | 30% | Redesign the onboarding checklist with personalized steps |
| **Big bets** | High effort, low confidence, high potential impact. New growth loops, major product changes, new channels. | 10% | Launch a referral program with two-sided incentives |

**Why this mix matters:** Quick wins keep the velocity high and deliver consistent incremental gains. Medium bets are where most learning happens. Big bets are where step-function growth comes from -- but most will fail, which is why they are only 10% of the portfolio. A team that only runs quick wins optimizes locally but misses breakthrough opportunities. A team that only runs big bets fails constantly and loses momentum.
